Big Tech earnings steadied markets, but higher yields and sticky inflation made July harder to dismiss.

The Market Wanted Proof

Hi,

Stocks finished the week higher, but the final print made July look calmer than it felt.

The S&P 500 gained about 1% for the week, the Nasdaq rose 1.6%, and the Dow added 1%. Even so, the S&P 500 still posted its first negative July since 2014. A strong closing week changed the mood, but it did not change the shape of the month.

Week of July 27-31, 2026

S&P 500: 7,489.72, up 1.0% for the week

Nasdaq: 25,373.85, up 1.6%

Dow: 52,485.03, up 1.0%

Brent crude: around $90 per barrel, lower than midweek levels but still elevated

What mattered

Two weeks ago, investors punished heavy AI capital spending. This week, they rewarded it. The spending plans did not suddenly become smaller. What changed was the evidence attached to the spending.

When revenue, cloud demand, margin discipline or operating leverage appeared beside the capex guidance, investors treated the spending as investment rather than leakage. Markets can tolerate enormous spending when they believe the money is widening an advantage. They become less forgiving when the spending looks like a tax required to stay in the race.

The difference between AI spending as risk and AI spending as reassurance is the proof sitting beside it.

The harder signal

The week's tension was not inside a single earnings report. It was between growth and inflation. The source draft points to slower GDP growth beside sticky core inflation, while long-dated Treasury yields moved higher.

That matters because higher long-term yields change the discount rate on future cash flows. It affects every asset, but especially companies whose valuation depends on profits arriving far into the future.

My take

This was not a clean risk-on week. It was not a broad rejection of technology. It was a market trying to reconcile strong corporate earnings with a less forgiving cost of capital.

When long yields rise, growth slows and inflation stays sticky, the hurdle rate for every investment decision moves higher. Businesses have to justify capital spending. Investors have to justify valuation. Managers have to justify growth that consumes more cash than it returns.

One lesson

Always check the period, not just the latest print. One strong sales week can hide a weak month. One good customer review can distract from deteriorating service. One profitable project can obscure a process that is becoming harder to run.

Good analysis starts by asking what the latest data point is sitting inside. Is it a reversal, a bounce, a continuation or noise? The answer changes how much weight the number deserves.
