Five Percent Changes the Math

Hi,

Stocks finished higher this week, but the most consequential move happened in bonds.

The S&P 500 gained 1.2%, the Nasdaq rose 2.1% and the Dow ended a three-week losing streak. Meanwhile, the 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.18%, its highest level since July 2007.

Week of September 21-25, 2026

S&P 500: 7,743.41, up 1.21%

Nasdaq: 27,068.72, up 2.06%

Dow: 51,828.62, up 0.28%

10-year Treasury: 5.18%, its highest close since July 2007

What mattered

A 5% Treasury yield gives expensive growth stocks real competition for capital. It reduces the present value of profits expected years from now and raises the return investors can demand before accepting equity risk.

That does not mean technology stocks must fall whenever yields rise. Strong earnings and genuinely useful products can overcome the valuation pressure. It does mean the burden of proof is higher. A compelling story now has to compete with an actual return available elsewhere.

A 5% Treasury yield does not make growth impossible. It makes the price paid for growth matter again.

My take

Meta gained almost 13% as interest in Muse accelerated, while Microsoft rose after presenting a more coherent Copilot built around Home, Code and Autopilot. Investors raised their expectations for parts of the AI ecosystem faster than the bond market raised the discount rate.

The harder test comes next. AI infrastructure requires enormous spending on chips, data centres, energy and networks. If long-term yields remain above 5%, the industry must prove that those investments can still produce attractive returns after the cost of financing rises.

One lesson

Before calling a run of good outcomes resilience, decide what would change your mind. Write down the evidence supporting your view and the evidence that would disprove it. Conviction is useful when it survives contact with facts. Without that test, it is only a story you have become comfortable repeating.
