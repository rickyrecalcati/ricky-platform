When Growth Starts Consuming Cash

Hi,

For three days, this looked like an ordinary week. Then Meta reported strong revenue growth, negative free cash flow and another enormous capital-spending bill.

The market's question changed almost immediately. Investors were no longer asking only whether the AI build-out would produce growth. They were asking who would fund it, how long the returns would take and what happened to cash in the meantime.

Week of October 19-23, 2026

S&P 500: 7,623.68, down 0.5%

Nasdaq: 25,955.34, down 0.7%

Russell 2000: 2,775.90, up 2.0%

Brent crude: above $116 a barrel, its highest level since 2022

What mattered

Meta reported $64.6 billion in quarterly revenue, up 26% from a year earlier. It also spent $34.91 billion on capital expenditure and recorded negative free cash flow of $2.12 billion.

The Nasdaq fell 2.6% on Thursday and the semiconductor index dropped 6.7%. Earnings were not broadly weak. The concern was that extraordinary growth may now require extraordinary amounts of cash before investors know whether the eventual returns justify the bill.

Last quarter the market asked whether AI spending would pay off. This week it asked who is paying for it, and the cash flow statement supplied the answer.

My take

Meta is not facing an immediate financing problem. It remains profitable and holds more than $82 billion in cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities. The important change is in the standard investors are applying.

Revenue growth no longer answers every question. The companies spending most aggressively on AI must also show that operating cash flow can keep pace, particularly while oil remains expensive and Treasury yields sit near 5%.

One lesson

Profit and cash are not the same thing. A project can appear profitable while consuming more cash than the rest of the business produces. Follow the money through the operation, ask who funds the gap and decide how long you are prepared to wait for the return.
