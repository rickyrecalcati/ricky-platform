Introduction

You Have a Second Estate

Most people only discover the size of a digital life when someone dies.

At first, the work looks familiar. There are calls to make, documents to find, accounts to notify and decisions to organise. Someone looks for the will. Someone checks the bank. Someone finds the insurance paperwork, the house documents, the car registration, the folder in the filing cabinet that has not been opened for years. None of this is easy, especially when grief is still fresh, but at least the shape of the task is recognisable.

Then the smaller problems begin to appear, and they are not really small at all.

The phone is locked. The family photos are somewhere in iCloud or Google Photos, but nobody knows the password. The email account cannot be opened. Two-factor authentication codes are being sent to a device nobody can unlock. Subscriptions continue charging. Bills arrive from companies nobody recognises. There may be a PayPal account, an investment app, a cloud storage account, airline points, old documents, private messages, social media profiles, cryptocurrency, a personal website, a domain name, an Amazon account, a password manager and years of family memories sitting behind screens no one can enter.

Nothing especially dramatic has gone wrong. That is what makes the problem so common. The person who died may have been organised in every traditional sense. Their paperwork may have been tidy. Their will may have been current. Their bank may have been known. Their house keys may have been labelled. The issue is not that they were careless. It is that one person understood how their digital life worked, and that person is no longer here to explain it.

For most of human history, an estate was made up of things that could be found. Land, money, jewellery, vehicles, furniture, paper records, letters, photographs and certificates left physical clues. Even when families did not know exactly what someone owned, they could begin by looking through rooms, drawers, safes, filing cabinets and mail. A physical estate could be messy, but it was usually visible.

A digital estate is different. It can be scattered across dozens of companies in different countries, protected by passwords, recovery codes, device locks, encryption, platform policies and email addresses no one else can access. Some parts may have financial value. Some may have emotional value. Some may have no value except that they need to be closed before they keep creating cost or confusion. Some should be preserved. Some should be transferred. Some should be deleted.

This book begins with a simple idea: you have a second estate.

It may not look like an estate. It may not feel like one. You may not think of your phone, email, cloud storage, financial apps, crypto wallets, social accounts, subscriptions and digital documents as things that belong in an estate plan. But they are part of what you leave behind. They are part of the life your family may one day have to locate, understand and deal with.

The mistake is thinking this is only about money. Money matters, of course. Bank accounts, investment platforms, payment apps, cryptocurrency, online businesses, royalties, domain names and digital products can all hold real value. A forgotten brokerage account may represent years of saving. A small website earning a modest monthly income may still be an asset. Airline points, store credit, app balances and refunds may not change a family's future, but they are still worth knowing about.

But the most valuable parts of a digital estate are not always financial. A folder containing twenty years of family photographs may be worth nothing commercially and still be irreplaceable. A video of a child learning to walk, a voice memo, a scan of an old letter, a family recipe, a set of travel photos, a personal journal, a half-finished manuscript or a carefully maintained archive of documents may matter more to the people left behind than almost anything with a price tag.

Some things can be recovered through paperwork. Others disappear because nobody knew they existed.

That is the strange bargain of modern life. We have made daily living easier by moving more of it online, but we have also made many things harder to find when we are gone. Our phones remember our passwords. Our email receives our statements. Our cloud storage holds our photographs. Our banking apps replace paper letters. Our investment platforms live behind login screens. Our identities are spread across services that may never send a physical document to the house.

Convenience creates invisibility. Invisibility becomes a problem when someone else has to deal with your affairs.

Sofia knows the mortgage provider but not the login. She knows there was life insurance but not where the policy document is stored. She knows family photos are "in the cloud" but not which cloud. She knows her husband invested but not which platforms he used. When unfamiliar charges appear on the bank statement, she has no easy way to tell whether they are important, fraudulent, recurring or harmless.

Then there is two-factor authentication. Even if someone knows the password, the account may ask for a code from the dead person's phone. The phone is locked. The email needed to reset the password is inaccessible. The password manager is protected by a master password nobody knows. The recovery codes may exist somewhere, but nobody knows where they were stored.

This is not a rare technical problem. It is becoming a normal estate problem.

A person can be financially responsible, technically competent and personally organised, yet still leave behind a digital mess. Traditional estate planning was built for a world of paper records, known institutions and physical assets. It was not designed for a life spread across encrypted devices, cloud platforms, private accounts and apps that may only exist as icons on a locked phone.

This book is not a legal textbook, and it is not a technical manual. It is a practical guide to help you organise the parts of your digital life that someone else may one day need to find. The goal is not to create a giant password spreadsheet, print your private life and place it in a drawer. In fact, that can create a different kind of risk. A document that gives your family access later could give a thief access now if it is stored carelessly.

A good digital estate plan should not expose your life while you are alive. It should create a safe, clear system so the right person can find what matters when they genuinely need to.

That distinction matters. Your family does not need access to everything today. They do not need to read your private messages, browse your old files or know every account balance while you are alive. They do not need your crypto seed phrase written in a notebook labelled "Bitcoin password." They do not need a single spreadsheet that turns one stolen folder into a complete identity-theft kit.

What they need is a map.

They need to know what exists, where instructions are stored and what should happen next. They need to know which accounts matter, which devices are important, where documents live, who to contact, what should be preserved, what should be transferred, what should be closed and what should be deleted. In some cases, they may need credentials. In many cases, they may not. Normal estate procedures, platform processes and legal authority may provide access without anyone logging in as the deceased person. But if no one knows an account exists, even the best legal process can miss it.

This book will help you build that map.

It is designed to be completed in a practical way, not admired as another piece of information. The aim is not to make you paranoid or turn your life into an administrative project. The aim is to help you create a system over a weekend, then maintain it with a short annual review.

You will learn what belongs in your digital estate. You will understand why your phone, email and password manager are more important than they appear. You will see the difference between knowing an account exists and having permission to access it. You will think through what should happen to photos, social media, subscriptions, online businesses, financial accounts, crypto, cloud files and personal documents.

You will also make decisions, and that may be the most useful part of the process. Not everything should be kept. Not everything should be transferred. Not everything should be opened. A digital estate plan is not only a preservation plan. It is also a privacy plan, a security plan and a kindness to the people who may one day have to deal with your affairs while grieving.

Some things should be saved. Some should be closed. Some should be passed on. Some should be deleted without being opened.

The point is to decide while you can, instead of leaving your family to guess later.

Laws and platform policies vary, so this book should sit beside proper legal and financial advice rather than replace it. But you do not need to solve every formal question today to reduce confusion for your family. You can still create an inventory. You can still identify important accounts. You can still organise documents. You can still choose what should happen to your photos. You can still record where instructions are stored. You can still make the invisible parts of your life easier to find.

To make the work less abstract, a few composite examples will appear throughout the book.

Sofia is forty-two. Her husband dies suddenly, leaving behind a locked phone, a shared mortgage, two children, a cloud photo library, several investment apps and a password manager she has never opened. Nothing about their life looked disorganised until she had to continue it without him.

Mark is sixty-eight. He is remarried, has adult children from a previous marriage and owns cryptocurrency, a small share portfolio, a domain name and a lifetime of photos. He is not trying to hide anything, but different people in his life would make different decisions if he left no instructions.

The Chen family runs a small online business built around a Shopify store, social media accounts, supplier portals, email marketing software and a payment processor. When one parent becomes seriously ill, the family discovers that the business is not held together by systems. It is held together by memory.

These people are not case studies from one household. They are composites drawn from common situations. Their value is not that they are unusual. Their value is that they are ordinary enough to recognise.

Most people delay this work because it feels uncomfortable. That is understandable. Nobody enjoys imagining their own absence, or picturing someone they love trying to unlock a phone after a funeral, or wondering whether years of photos could vanish because nobody knows where they are stored. It is easier to assume someone will figure it out.

Maybe they will. But they will already have enough to carry.

A digital estate plan is not really about technology. It is about reducing the burden on people you care about. It is about making sure important things can be found, assets are not missed, memories are not lost and private material is handled with intention. It is about leaving fewer mysteries behind.

You do not need to be wealthy for this to matter. You only need to have a phone, an email address, some accounts, some memories and people who would be left trying to understand them.

That is almost everyone.

Before we start building the system, ask yourself one question:

If you disappeared tomorrow, how much of your digital life could your family actually find?

Not access. Not manage. Not close. Just find.

If the honest answer is "not much," you are not alone. That is exactly where this book begins.

If You Are Already Handling This for Someone Else

Most of this book is written for the person who still has time to plan. But not everyone arrives here early. Some readers will open this book because someone has already died, a parent has become seriously unwell, a partner can no longer manage their affairs, or a business is suddenly depending on accounts no one else understands.

If that is you, do not start by trying to read the whole book perfectly. Start by stabilising the situation.

Your first job is not to solve the entire digital estate. Your first job is to stop avoidable loss.

Begin with devices. Do not factory reset phones, laptops or tablets. Do not discard old devices. Do not cancel phone plans until you understand whether they receive two-factor authentication codes. Keep chargers, SIM cards, hardware keys, external drives and notebooks together. A device that looks old may be the only trusted device for an important account.

Next, protect email. Email is often the index of the digital estate. It can reveal banks, insurers, subscriptions, cloud storage, domains, payment processors, investment platforms, tax records and old accounts. If you cannot access the email, look for statements, notifications on devices, saved browser profiles, password managers, printed documents and official provider processes.

Then pause obvious risks. Keep cloud storage active until photos and documents are understood. Keep domain names and website hosting active until you know whether they control email, business identity or income. Keep password manager subscriptions active. Keep business software active until customer, payment, payroll and tax records have been checked.

Use this order:

  1. Preserve devices and chargers.
  2. Identify primary email accounts.
  3. Check bank statements for recurring charges.
  4. Look for cloud storage and photo libraries.
  5. Identify password managers and authenticator apps.
  6. List financial providers without trying to log in.
  7. Pause before cancelling phone plans, domains, cloud storage or business tools.
  8. Contact official providers where estate documents are required.
  9. Ask for technical help before touching crypto, hardware wallets or recovery phrases.
  10. Write down every provider, account and clue as you discover it.

Sofia did not need a perfect inventory in the first week after her husband died. She needed to know not to cancel his phone plan, not to reset the laptop, not to close the cloud storage account and not to assume every app on the phone was unimportant. Those four decisions protected more value than any beautifully formatted plan could have done in that moment.

If you are already in the middle of the cleanup, the chapters ahead will still help. Read them out of order if you need to. Start with email, devices, money, subscriptions and photos. Come back to the full plan later.

For now, preserve first. Decide after you understand what you are holding.

Chapter 1

Your Digital Life Is Bigger Than You Think

Most people underestimate their digital estate because they are looking for the wrong thing.

They look for money.

They think about bank accounts, investments, property, insurance and maybe a few important documents saved somewhere on a computer. If they have cryptocurrency, a business website or online income, they may include those too. Everything else feels too ordinary to count. Email does not feel like an asset. A phone does not feel like part of an estate. Cloud photos do not feel like property. Subscriptions, reward points, social accounts, stored files and digital purchases seem more like background noise than something a family may one day need to understand.

A digital estate is not only made up of things that have a dollar value.

Mark has a watch he wants his eldest son to inherit. It is listed in his will, insured, photographed and kept in a drawer his wife knows about. He also has more than 40,000 family photos across iCloud, an old Google account and two external drives in the garage. The photos include birthdays, holidays, old houses, school years, grandparents, children, weddings, ordinary Sunday mornings and videos that were never backed up anywhere else.

The watch is visible, so it gets planned for. The photos are invisible, so they are treated as if someone will simply know where they are. If Mark died tomorrow, his family would probably find the watch in an afternoon. The photos could take weeks to locate, and some might never be recovered at all.

Ask the family which one would hurt more to lose, and the answer may not be the one with the clearer price tag.

This is why the first step in building a digital estate plan is not writing down passwords. It is widening your definition of what belongs in the plan at all. Before you decide what should happen to something, you need to know it exists. Before anyone can preserve, close, transfer or delete an account, someone needs to be able to find it. The inventory comes before the instructions.

Most people's digital lives are much larger than they think because modern life quietly spreads itself across devices, platforms and accounts. You may not remember creating half of them. Some were opened years ago for a single purpose and never closed. Some are attached to old email addresses. Some charge once a year. Some hold documents, memories, private information or access to something else. Many look unimportant until something goes wrong.

Your digital estate begins with the obvious things: your phone, computer, tablet, email and password manager. These are not just devices or tools. They are gateways. Your phone may unlock banking apps, messaging, authentication codes, photos, cloud storage and password resets. Your email may reveal almost every service you have ever used. Your password manager may be the closest thing you have to a master index of your online life.

Then there are the accounts that hold money or connect to money. Online banking, digital banks, credit cards, PayPal, payment apps, buy-now-pay-later services, investment platforms, brokerage accounts, managed funds, employee share plans, cryptocurrency exchanges, crypto wallets, crowdfunding accounts and overseas platforms. Some of these may be known to your family. Others may exist only as an app on your phone or a monthly email statement in your inbox.

Not all financial value looks like a bank balance. Airline points, hotel rewards, store credit, cashback accounts, gift cards, prepaid cards, app balances and refunds can all be part of the picture. Individually, they may not matter much. Together, they may be worth finding. Even when they are not transferable, your family should not have to guess whether they exist.

There are also the accounts that hold your memories. iCloud Photos. Google Photos. Dropbox. OneDrive. Old external hard drives. SD cards. USB drives. Old phones sitting in drawers. Family videos saved in messaging apps. Shared albums. Scanned documents. Voice memos. These can be among the most important parts of a digital estate because they are often irreplaceable. If money is lost, there may be records. If photos are lost, there may be nothing.

Social media creates a different kind of question. Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, X, TikTok, YouTube, personal blogs, forums and online communities may not feel like assets, but they are still part of your public identity. Some platforms allow memorialisation. Some allow deletion. Some have legacy contact features. Some may be difficult to access without formal processes. Your family may need to know whether you want those profiles preserved, closed or left alone.

Then come the subscriptions and services that keep running because nobody notices them. Streaming accounts, cloud storage, software tools, apps, news subscriptions, fitness memberships, design tools, website plugins, domain renewals, hosting plans, accounting software, online courses, meal services, utilities and shopping memberships. These may not be emotionally important, but they create administrative work. A subscription that quietly charges $12 a month is easy to ignore while you are alive and strangely frustrating for someone else to unwind after you are gone.

Digital documents are another category people overlook. Tax records, insurance policies, scanned IDs, contracts, certificates, business records, medical directives, property documents, loan papers, receipts, warranties and family files may be scattered across email attachments, Downloads folders, cloud drives, screenshots and old laptops. You may know where everything is because you created the mess gradually. Someone else will only see a maze.

If you own or run anything online, the estate becomes even wider. Domain names, websites, newsletters, e-commerce stores, creator accounts, affiliate programs, Amazon KDP accounts, digital products, online courses, royalties, advertising accounts and business email systems may all matter. A small project that earns $200 a month may not feel like much when you are alive, but it can still be an asset. It can also become a liability if hosting renews, customers keep writing, or revenue keeps landing in an account nobody knows about.

Even digital purchases deserve some attention, although they can be misunderstood. Music libraries, movies, e-books, games, apps and software are often governed by licences rather than traditional ownership. You may have paid for access without owning something that can be transferred like a physical object. That does not make them irrelevant, but it does mean they need to be treated differently from assets such as bank accounts or domain names.

The point is not that every digital account deserves the same level of planning. It does not. Your family does not need a detailed instruction sheet for every streaming service, shopping account or app you tried once in 2016. A digital estate plan should not become a museum of every login you have ever created.

The point is that you need to know which things matter.

A useful way to think about your digital estate is to separate it into five kinds of value.

The first is financial value. This includes anything that contains money, produces income, points to money, or helps locate money. Bank accounts, investment platforms, payment apps, crypto, reward balances, royalties, business revenue and online stores belong here.

The second is emotional value. This includes photos, videos, messages, voice recordings, family documents, creative work and anything your loved ones may want to preserve because it carries memory rather than market value.

The third is practical value. These are the accounts and documents that help someone administer your life: email, tax records, insurance documents, bills, subscriptions, cloud drives, utilities, receipts, contracts and household accounts.

The fourth is identity value. This includes social media, public profiles, websites, blogs, usernames, professional accounts and anything that represents you publicly or privately online.

The fifth is security value. These are the keys to the rest of the system: phones, computers, password managers, recovery emails, authenticator apps, backup codes, hardware keys and trusted devices. These may not be assets in the normal sense, but they determine whether other assets can be found or accessed.

This framework helps because it stops the conversation from becoming only about passwords. Some digital assets need to be accessed. Some only need to be located. Some need to be closed. Some need to be preserved. Some need to be protected from access. Some simply need to be mentioned so your family does not spend months wondering what they missed.

For example, your primary email account has practical and security value. It may not contain money, but it can reveal financial accounts, subscriptions, receipts, legal documents and password reset links. Your phone has security value because it may receive two-factor authentication codes. Your cloud photo library has emotional value. Your domain name may have financial, identity and business value. Your Netflix account probably has low estate value, but it may still need to be closed if it keeps charging.

Once you start seeing your digital life this way, the size of it becomes clearer. Not because every account is important, but because the important accounts are mixed in with everything else.

That is what makes digital estate planning difficult. The valuable, emotional and urgent things are not neatly separated from the trivial things. Your inbox may contain both a receipt for a forgotten streaming subscription and the only clue to an investment platform. Your phone may hold both old takeaway apps and the authentication method for your bank. Your cloud drive may hold useless screenshots beside insurance documents. Your password manager may contain hundreds of entries, only twenty of which truly matter.

A good digital estate plan does not try to make everything equally important. It helps someone separate signal from noise.

That begins with a scan.

Not a perfect inventory. Not a full weekend project. Not a password list. Just a first pass at what exists.

The purpose of the first scan is to overcome invisibility. You are not solving the estate yet. You are not deciding what should happen to every account. You are not writing down credentials. You are simply proving to yourself that your digital life is bigger than the handful of accounts that came to mind when you first thought about it.

Most people will be surprised by what they remember in ten minutes.

They will remember an old PayPal account. A second email address. A cloud drive attached to a previous phone. An investment app they opened years ago. A domain name they bought for an idea that never became a business. A Dropbox folder with family documents. A rewards account. A crypto exchange login. A hard drive in a drawer. A subscription charged annually. A social profile they no longer use but never deleted.

That surprise is useful. It shows the work is worth doing.

You do not need to panic about the length of the list. A long list does not mean your estate is out of control. It simply means your life has moved through the same digital systems everyone else uses. The goal is not to become perfectly organised in one sitting. The goal is to create enough visibility that the next decisions become possible.

For now, resist the urge to fix everything. Do not start resetting passwords. Do not start closing accounts. Do not move crypto. Do not print secret recovery codes. Do not create a master spreadsheet full of sensitive information because you suddenly feel behind. Just list, because a map begins with names.

Chapter 2

What Happens to Your Accounts When You Die?

One of the most common assumptions about digital life is also one of the most dangerous: if you pay for something, use something, or log in to something, you own it in the same way you own a physical object.

That feels reasonable. You bought the movie. You paid for the e-book. You created the account. You uploaded the photos. You built the profile. You registered the domain. You opened the investment app. You used the service for years. In ordinary language, people say things like "my Netflix account," "my Apple library," "my photos," "my email," "my Instagram," "my crypto," "my website."

The problem is that ordinary language and platform rules do not always mean the same thing.

A physical book on your shelf is easy to understand. You bought it. You can lend it, sell it, give it away or leave it to someone in your will. A digital e-book may feel similar because you paid money and can read it whenever you like, but what you bought may be a licence to access that content under a platform's terms. That licence may not be transferable after death. The same may be true for music, films, games, software and other digital purchases.

This does not mean digital purchases are worthless or irrelevant. It means they do not always behave like the physical possessions they replaced.

That distinction matters because estate planning often depends on the difference between owning an asset and having access to an account.

In the physical world, ownership and access are often closely linked. If someone owns a watch, the watch can usually be found, valued and transferred. If someone owns a house, there are titles, records, legal processes and institutions that recognise the asset. If someone owns shares through a traditional broker, the asset may be transferred through estate procedures even if nobody knows the online password.

In the digital world, the login screen can make everything look the same.

A bank account, a Netflix profile, a crypto wallet, a Dropbox folder, an Apple ID, a domain name, a social media profile and a cloud photo library may all require an email address, password and verification code. But behind those login screens are very different kinds of rights, obligations and risks.

Some accounts point to assets you own. Some accounts are services you use. Some contain data you created. Some give you access to licensed content. Some are governed by financial regulation. Some are governed mostly by contract. Some can be transferred. Some can be closed. Some can be memorialised. Some may be inaccessible without a formal legal process. Some may disappear if the wrong information is lost.

A digital estate plan works better when you stop treating every account as the same kind of thing.

The first category is assets. These are things that may have legal or financial value beyond the account used to view them. Bank balances, shares, managed funds, domain names, online businesses, royalties, digital products, payment balances and some forms of cryptocurrency can fall into this category. The account matters because it helps identify and manage the asset, but the underlying value may exist separately from the login.

For example, an online brokerage account is not valuable because the app is valuable. It is valuable because the account may hold shares, cash or other investments. Your family may not need your password to receive those assets. In fact, logging in as you may create legal or practical problems depending on the platform and jurisdiction. What they need first is to know the account exists, which provider holds it and where the relevant documentation can be found.

The second category is licences. These are rights to use or access something under terms set by a company. Digital music, films, e-books, apps, games and software often fall into this category. You may think of them as purchases, and in daily life that is how they feel. But the legal structure may be closer to permission than ownership.

This can be uncomfortable because it exposes a quiet trade we have made for convenience. Physical collections used to sit on shelves. Digital collections often sit inside ecosystems. The platform makes access easy while you are alive, but after death the question becomes more complicated: can the account be transferred, can the content be accessed by someone else, or does the right to use it end with the account holder?

The answer depends on the platform and the terms in place at the time. That is why a digital estate plan should not promise that every digital purchase can be passed on like a box of DVDs. It should simply record where important digital libraries exist and what the reader would like to happen if access is possible.

The third category is accounts. An account is the relationship between a person and a platform. It may contain assets, data, messages, purchases, subscriptions, permissions or identity. But the account itself may not always be transferable.

Social media is a good example. A Facebook account, Instagram profile or LinkedIn profile may contain photographs, messages, contacts, business history and public identity. But that does not mean another person can simply inherit the account and continue using it as if nothing happened. Some platforms have legacy contact systems. Some allow memorialisation. Some may allow deletion after documentation is provided. Some provide limited access to certain data. Others may be more restrictive.

The account may feel personal because it is personal. But personal does not always mean transferable.

The fourth category is data. This includes photos, videos, documents, emails, messages, files, notes and other information stored inside accounts or devices. Data can be valuable even when the account itself is not. A cloud storage account may be a paid service, but the documents inside it may include tax records, family photos, business files, contracts or creative work. An email account may not be an asset in the financial sense, but the messages inside it may reveal insurance policies, investments, invoices, subscriptions and personal history.

Data is also where privacy becomes important. Not everything stored digitally should automatically be opened, copied or preserved. Some information may involve other people. Some may be deeply personal. Some may be useful only for administration. Some may be something you would prefer to have deleted. A good digital estate plan should not treat data as one big pile. It should help someone understand what matters and how it should be handled.

The fifth category is contractual access. This is the permission created by a company's terms of service. When you create an account, you usually agree to rules about how it can be used, who can access it and what happens if the account holder dies. Most people never read these terms, but the terms still matter.

This is why simply giving someone your password is not the same thing as giving them legal authority. It may work technically. It may even feel sensible. But depending on the account, platform and jurisdiction, accessing someone else's account after death may breach terms of service or create legal risk. Financial accounts, medical records, business systems and private communications require particular care.

This book is not asking you to become a lawyer. It is asking you to understand that access is not the same as ownership.

That idea is important enough to repeat.

Access is the ability to get into an account.

Ownership is the legal or beneficial right to the asset, information or property connected to it.

Those two things often overlap while you are alive, but they can separate after death.

Consider three examples.

A streaming account may be accessible if someone knows the password, but there may be nothing meaningful to inherit. The account is mainly a subscription service. The practical instruction may be: cancel it.

An online brokerage account may be inaccessible to family members without formal estate procedures, but the assets inside it may absolutely belong to the estate. The practical instruction may be: do not log in, but make sure the executor knows the provider, account type and documentation location.

A self-custodied crypto wallet may be different again. There may be no customer service department, no password reset process and no institution able to recognise a death certificate. If the recovery phrase is lost, the asset may be lost. If the recovery phrase is exposed too casually, the asset may be stolen. The practical instruction may be: record that the wallet exists, explain where the recovery instructions are stored, and keep the recovery material secured separately.

Each case involves a digital account or digital system. Each requires a different response.

This is why a digital estate plan should avoid two extremes.

The first extreme is assuming everything can simply be passed on. That is not true. Some accounts may be closed rather than inherited. Some digital purchases may not transfer. Some platforms may offer only limited options. Some content may remain locked behind policies your family cannot easily change.

The second extreme is assuming nothing can be done. That is also not true. Families can often locate assets, close accounts, recover documents, preserve photos, manage subscriptions, transfer domains, deal with financial institutions and follow platform processes. The work becomes much easier when the person who died left clear information about what exists and what should happen.

Clarity does not guarantee access, but it improves the odds of a sane process.

A useful estate plan speaks in practical language. It does not need to resolve every legal detail inside the inventory. Instead, it should help the right person know what kind of thing they are dealing with.

Is this an asset with financial value?

Is this a service that should be cancelled?

Is this an account containing memories?

Is this a public identity that should be memorialised or deleted?

Is this a device or email account that helps unlock other parts of the estate?

Is this private material that should be handled carefully or not opened at all?

Those questions are more useful than a giant list of usernames and passwords.

The platform rules will still matter. Laws will still vary by country. Professional advice may still be needed for estate documents, financial assets and legal authority. But your family will no longer be starting from nothing.

This is where many people misunderstand the purpose of a digital estate plan. They think the plan must give immediate access to everything. That is not the goal. The goal is to give the right person enough information to take the next lawful step.

For a bank account, the next step may be contacting the institution with estate documents.

For a social media profile, the next step may be requesting memorialisation or deletion.

For a cloud photo account, the next step may be following the platform's legacy access process or locating an authorised backup.

For a domain name, the next step may be transferring control through the registrar.

For a subscription, the next step may be cancellation.

For a crypto wallet, the next step may involve carefully following recovery instructions without exposing keys.

The better the map, the less guessing your family has to do.

There is also a privacy benefit. If your plan clearly says what should happen to different categories of accounts, your family does not have to search everything blindly. They do not need to open every folder, read every message or inspect every account just to work out whether something matters. You can give them direction without giving them unnecessary access.

This is especially important for email. Email may reveal almost everything, but it may also contain years of private communication. Your family may need to know where important records are, but that does not mean every message should become family property. A clear plan can say: use this account to locate financial and administrative records, then close it after the estate is settled. Or it can say: preserve certain folders. Or it can say: delete personal correspondence without review.

The same applies to cloud storage, social media, notes apps, personal documents and messages. A digital estate plan should respect the person who died as well as the people left behind.

This is the delicate balance of the book: making important things findable without turning a private life into an open archive.

The practical takeaway is simple: every account in your digital estate should eventually be understood by type. You do not need to do this perfectly today. But as you build your inventory, start noticing the difference between assets, licences, accounts, data and access.

A password does not tell your family what something is. A map does.

Chapter 3

What Your Family Will Actually Need

A digital estate plan can easily become too big for its own good.

Once you start listing accounts, devices, cloud storage, subscriptions, financial platforms, old email addresses, social media profiles and documents, the natural instinct is to keep adding more detail. Every account feels like it needs a password. Every folder feels like it needs an explanation. Every app feels like it needs a decision. Before long, the plan starts to look less like a map and more like a second job.

That is where many people stop.

They begin with good intentions, open a spreadsheet, list twenty accounts, realise there may be another hundred, and quietly abandon the whole project. Not because they do not care, but because the task begins to feel impossible. A digital life is messy. It grows without permission. It contains old accounts, forgotten subscriptions, half-used services, duplicate files, private material and things that mattered once but no longer matter at all.

The answer is not to document everything in equal detail.

Your family does not need everything.

They need the right information at the right level.

That distinction is the heart of this chapter.

A useful digital estate plan is not a giant password list. It is not a complete archive of your online life. It is not an invitation for someone to open every account, read every message, browse every file and manage every digital trace you leave behind. In many cases, that would be unnecessary. In some cases, it would be inappropriate. In a few cases, it could create risk.

What your family needs is a practical map that helps them answer three questions:

What exists?

Where can it be found?

What should happen next?

Those three questions sound simple, but they solve most of the confusion. When someone dies, the people left behind are usually not trying to understand the entire person through every account they ever created. They are trying to deal with urgent practical matters while grieving. They need to find financial accounts, preserve important memories, stop unnecessary charges, locate documents, manage public profiles, protect identity and avoid missing anything important.

A clear map helps them do that without forcing them to become detectives.

The first thing your family needs is to know that something exists.

This sounds almost too basic to mention, but it is the most common failure point. An account that no one knows about may as well be invisible. A brokerage account, crypto wallet, online savings account, cloud archive, website, domain name or royalty account can hold real value and still be missed simply because nobody knew to look for it.

Knowing an account exists does not mean someone needs immediate access. It means the account is on the map. It means the person handling your affairs knows there is something to investigate, verify or include in the estate process.

This matters most with financial accounts, investments, crypto, online businesses and important documents. Your family may not need your login credentials for a brokerage account, but they do need to know which brokerage holds the account. They may not need to open your business email, but they do need to know that a website earns revenue, has hosting costs and receives customer enquiries. They may not need to access a crypto wallet on day one, but they absolutely need to know whether one exists and where the recovery instructions are stored.

Existence is the first level of the map.

The second level is knowing how to locate something.

This is different from knowing it exists. If you write "life insurance" on a piece of paper, that is useful but incomplete. Which provider? Policy number? Where are the documents? Who is the broker? Is the latest version stored in email, a cloud drive, a filing cabinet or an online portal?

The same applies to digital assets. "Photos in the cloud" is not enough. Which cloud? Which account? Which email address is attached to it? Is there a shared family library? Are there local backups? Is there an external hard drive? Are the most important folders named clearly?

Location is about giving someone enough information to start in the right place.

For example, your plan might say:

Primary family photos are stored in Google Photos under my main Gmail account. A second backup is on the external hard drive labelled "Family Archive" in the study cupboard. Do not delete either account until the photos have been reviewed and copied.

That instruction does not reveal a password. It does not expose private material unnecessarily. But it gives your family a starting point. It tells them what matters, where to look and what not to do too quickly.

The third level is knowing how to access something.

This is where people often jump too early. They assume the estate plan must contain every password, recovery code and credential. That feels thorough, but it can also be dangerous. As we will discuss in later chapters, a document containing all your passwords can become a serious security risk if it is stolen, copied, emailed, misplaced or discovered by the wrong person.

Access should be treated carefully. Some accounts require it. Many do not. Some should be accessed only through formal estate processes. Others may need emergency instructions. Some should not be opened at all unless there is a clear reason.

Your family may need access to a password manager, but not if the plan explains a safer emergency access process. They may need access to a phone, but only because it sits early in the recovery chain. They may need access to cloud photos, but not every private folder. They may need instructions for a crypto wallet, but the recovery phrase itself should not sit casually in a normal inventory.

Access is the highest level of the map because it carries the most risk. It should be used where it is genuinely needed, not as the default setting for every account.

This gives us a simple framework for the rest of the book:

Know it exists.

Know how to locate it.

Know how to access it, only where necessary.

Most accounts do not need all three levels.

A streaming subscription may only need to be known and cancelled. A bank account may need to be known and handled through formal estate procedures. A cloud photo library may need to be located and preserved. A crypto wallet may require careful access instructions. A private journal may need to be located only so it can be deleted unread according to your wishes.

This is how you avoid turning your digital estate plan into an overwhelming password spreadsheet. You decide what level of information each asset actually needs.

Let's make this more concrete.

Your primary email account probably needs all three levels. Your family should know it exists, know which email address it is, understand why it matters and know where lawful access or recovery instructions are stored. Email can reveal statements, subscriptions, account registrations, receipts, documents and recovery links. It may be one of the most important accounts in the entire plan.

Your Netflix account probably does not need all three levels. It may only need to be listed as a subscription to cancel, if it is listed at all. Your family does not need to inherit your watchlist. They do not need detailed access instructions. They just need to stop the charge if it continues.

Your brokerage account needs to be known and locatable. Your family should know the provider, account type, approximate purpose and documentation location. But they may not need your password. In many cases, they should contact the provider through the normal estate process rather than logging in as you.

Your password manager is different. It may be the gateway to many other accounts. It deserves careful planning, but not careless disclosure. Your family may need to know that you use one, which provider you use, whether an emergency access feature is set up, where recovery instructions are stored and who is authorised to begin that process. That is very different from printing the master password and taping it inside a folder.

Your social media accounts may need decisions more than access. Do you want them memorialised, deleted, preserved, ignored or reviewed later? Does one account contain important business contacts? Does another contain private messages you would prefer no one to read? The instruction may be less about logging in and more about what outcome you want.

Your family photos need preservation instructions. They need to know where the main library is stored, whether there are backups, which folders matter most and who should receive copies. They may not need access to everything immediately, but they do need to know not to close an account or wipe a device before the memories have been secured.

Your crypto holdings, if you have any, require a different level of discipline. Your family needs to know whether crypto exists. They need to know whether it is held on an exchange or self-custodied. They need to know where recovery instructions are stored. But they should not be handed sensitive recovery material in a casual document that could be stolen or misunderstood. Crypto is one of the clearest examples of why "more detail" is not always safer.

A good plan gives enough information to reduce confusion without creating unnecessary exposure. That is the balance you are trying to build.

To build that balance, each item in your digital estate should eventually be marked with three decisions: FIND, ACCESS and ACTION.

FIND means the account or asset needs to be discoverable. Your family should know it exists and know enough to identify it. This is the minimum level for anything important. If something has financial value, emotional value, administrative value or security value, it should usually be findable.

ACCESS means someone may need a way to get into the account, device, system or storage location. This should be reserved for accounts where access is genuinely required and appropriate. It should also point to a secure access method rather than casually exposing credentials.

ACTION means the plan should say what should happen next. Preserve it. Transfer it. Close it. Delete it. Contact the provider. Follow legal process. Copy the files. Leave it alone. The action may be simple, but it removes guesswork.

It also respects privacy.

People sometimes avoid digital estate planning because they imagine it means handing someone access to everything. They picture a spouse, child, sibling or executor reading private messages, browsing old files, opening accounts that were never meant to be shared and making sense of years of personal material. That fear is understandable.

But a digital estate plan can actually protect privacy more effectively than silence.

Without a plan, your family may have to search blindly. They may open accounts because they do not know where important documents are. They may read folders because they cannot tell whether they contain financial information. They may preserve everything because they are afraid to delete something meaningful. They may close accounts too quickly because they do not realise photos or documents are inside.

A clear plan lets you separate the administrative from the personal. It can tell your family where the insurance documents are without asking them to read your email history. It can tell them which folders hold family photos and which folders should remain private. It can identify business accounts without exposing personal conversations. It can say, plainly, that some material should be deleted unread.

Privacy requires instruction.

Silence does not protect your digital life. It simply leaves other people to guess.

There is another benefit to this framework: it helps you avoid unnecessary maintenance. If every account needed a full set of passwords, notes, balances and instructions, your plan would become outdated almost immediately. Passwords change. Balances change. Subscriptions come and go. Devices are replaced. Platforms update their policies. A plan that tries to document everything at maximum detail becomes fragile.

A map is more durable.

Instead of recording constantly changing balances, you record the provider and account type. Instead of writing every password, you record the secure process for access. Instead of listing every document separately, you record the folder structure. Instead of updating every subscription weekly, you note the main places subscriptions can be found, such as email receipts, bank statements and app store subscriptions.

The goal is not perfect information. It is useful information.

Your family does not need a museum catalogue. They need directions.

This is especially important because the people handling your estate may not be calm, rested or technically confident. They may be grieving. They may be dealing with funeral arrangements, legal paperwork, family tension, financial pressure and hundreds of small decisions. A digital estate plan should be written for that version of the reader, not for an ideal administrator sitting peacefully with unlimited time.

Simple beats clever.

Clear beats complete.

Useful beats impressive.

If someone can open your plan and quickly understand which accounts matter, where to begin and what to avoid, the plan is doing its job.

Chapter 4

Passwords Without the Password List

The first solution most people think of is also one of the riskiest.

They imagine sitting down one afternoon, opening a spreadsheet and typing every password they use. Email. Banking. Cloud storage. Social media. Investment platforms. Subscriptions. Shopping accounts. Password manager. Phone passcode. Maybe even recovery codes and crypto seed phrases, if they have them.

Then they imagine printing it, placing it in a folder, and telling someone they trust where to find it.

It feels responsible. It feels organised. It feels like the obvious answer to the problem this book is trying to solve. If passwords are what stand between your family and your digital life, surely the answer is to write them down.

The problem is that a password list can solve one problem by creating another.

A folder containing every password is not just an estate document. It is a target. If it is stolen, photographed, copied, emailed, misplaced or discovered by the wrong person, the estate plan becomes the security vulnerability. The very thing designed to protect your family later may expose you while you are alive.

Imagine someone who takes digital estate planning seriously. They create a folder labelled "Important Information." Inside it are printed pages with email passwords, banking passwords, cloud storage passwords, investment logins, social media credentials, recovery notes and device passcodes. They place it in a filing cabinet at home. They tell their partner it exists. They feel relieved.

A year later, the house is burgled. The jewellery is replaceable. The television is replaceable. The laptop may be encrypted. But the folder in the filing cabinet contains everything. Not just a clue. Not just an instruction. Everything.

The person who stole it may now have access to email, banking, cloud files, password resets, identity documents, financial accounts and private information. Even if the passwords are changed quickly, the damage may already have started. The estate plan has become a master key left in plain sight.

This does not mean passwords should never be written down. It means they should not be collected casually, stored carelessly or treated as if the only risk is your family being locked out after death. There are two risks to manage at the same time: your family may one day need access, and the wrong person must not get access before then.

A good digital estate plan respects both.

To do that, it helps to understand why passwords are such a fragile part of modern life.

Most people have too many accounts and too few good passwords. They reuse the same password across multiple sites because remembering unique passwords feels impossible. They make small variations when a website asks for something different. They use names, dates, favourite places, old addresses or patterns that feel memorable. They save passwords in browsers, notes apps, contacts, notebooks and old messages to themselves.

This is understandable. It is also risky.

Password reuse is one of the simplest ways one breach becomes many breaches. If an old shopping site leaks your email and password, and you used that same password for email, banking, cloud storage or social media, the problem spreads. The original account may not matter much. The reused password does.

That is why unique passwords matter. A strong password used in one place is much safer than a clever password reused everywhere. The problem is that unique passwords are hard for humans to remember at scale. No normal person can reliably memorise long, random, unique passwords for every important account.

That is why password managers exist.

A password manager is a tool that stores your passwords in an encrypted vault. Instead of remembering hundreds of passwords, you remember one master password. The password manager can create long, unique passwords for each account and fill them in when needed. Good password habits become possible because you are no longer relying on memory.

For digital estate planning, a password manager can be extremely useful. It can become the organised centre of your credential system. It can reduce password reuse. It can reveal which accounts exist. It can help you keep important credentials current without maintaining a printed list that becomes outdated the moment a password changes.

But a password manager also changes the estate question.

If your password manager holds the keys to your digital life, what happens if nobody can access it when you are gone?

This is where many people get stuck. They either avoid password managers because they fear creating one point of failure, or they use one but never think through emergency access. Both approaches have problems. A password manager without a recovery plan can lock everyone out. A handwritten password list without security can let the wrong person in.

The answer is not to reject password managers or print every secret. The answer is to create a credential access plan.

A credential access plan explains how an authorised person could eventually obtain access to the accounts that genuinely require it. It does not have to contain every password itself. In fact, it usually should not. Its job is to explain the path.

That path might include:

  • which password manager you use
  • who should request emergency access
  • whether the password manager has an emergency access feature
  • where recovery instructions are stored
  • where a sealed master password backup is kept, if you choose to create one
  • which devices may be needed
  • which email address controls password resets
  • which accounts should be handled through formal estate procedures instead of login access

The difference between a password list and an access plan is important. A password list says: here are all the keys. An access plan says: here is how the right person can find the right key, at the right time, in the right way.

That second approach is slower, but safer. It reduces the chance that one stolen document exposes everything. It also makes the plan more durable. Passwords change constantly. Access methods change less often. If your inventory says, "All account credentials are stored in 1Password. Emergency access instructions are kept in the estate folder. The sealed master password backup is stored in the safe," you do not need to update the estate folder every time Netflix, Dropbox or your bank asks you to change a password.

You update the system, not every individual secret.

There are several ways to structure this, and the right answer depends on your life, your risk level and the people you trust.

Some password managers offer emergency access features. These allow you to nominate a trusted person who can request access to your vault. Usually, there is a waiting period. If you are alive and receive the request, you can deny it. If you are incapacitated or dead and no one denies the request, access may eventually be granted. The exact features differ between providers, so you should review how your chosen service handles this.

Emergency access can be useful because it avoids placing the master password directly in a folder. But it still requires planning. The trusted person needs to know they have been nominated. They need to know which service you use. They may need their own account. They need to understand when it is appropriate to request access. If the feature depends on your email, phone or device, the recovery chain still matters.

Some people prefer a physical backup. This might be a sealed envelope containing the password manager master password or recovery instructions, stored in a safe, with a lawyer, in a bank safe deposit box, or another secure location. This can work, but it should be treated carefully. The envelope should not sit casually in a desk drawer. It should not contain more than necessary. It should be reviewed when passwords or systems change. Someone trusted should know it exists, but not necessarily be able to access it casually.

A physical backup is not automatically unsafe. It depends on how it is designed. A sticky note under the keyboard is unsafe. A sealed instruction stored securely as part of a broader estate process may be reasonable. The difference is control.

Another option is splitting information. One location may contain the instruction that a password manager exists. Another may contain the sealed master password. Another trusted person may know where the physical backup is held. This reduces the risk that one discovered document gives immediate access to everything. It also increases complexity, so it should not be overdone. A plan that is too clever can become unusable.

The aim is not to build a spy movie system. The aim is to prevent both chaos and carelessness.

For most people, a good solution will be simple:

  • use a reputable password manager
  • use long, unique passwords
  • protect the password manager with a strong master password
  • turn on two-factor authentication
  • document which password manager is used
  • set up emergency access if appropriate
  • store recovery instructions securely
  • tell the right person that the plan exists

That is already far better than scattered passwords, repeated passwords and no plan at all.

There is one place passwords generally should not go: directly in your will.

A will may become part of a legal process. Depending on where you live, it may be seen by people beyond the immediate person you trust. It may be copied, stored, filed or handled by professionals and family members. It may not remain private in the way you assume. Even if it does, it is not usually designed to be updated every time a password changes.

Your will can refer to the existence of digital estate instructions. It can name relevant people where legally appropriate. It can express wishes. It can work with other estate documents. But it should not usually contain live passwords, recovery codes, seed phrases or sensitive credentials.

Sensitive access information should be kept separately and securely, with clear instructions about where it is stored and who is authorised to use it.

This is especially important for email. As later chapters will explain, your primary email account may be the account behind everything. It can reset passwords, reveal account registrations and receive financial statements. If someone can access your email, they may be able to access far more than email. That makes email credentials particularly sensitive.

The same is true for your phone passcode, password manager master password, authenticator app, cloud storage and crypto recovery material. These are not ordinary passwords. They are gateways. Treating them casually can expose the whole estate.

This does not mean your family should be left helpless. It means the instructions should match the risk.

For example, your plan might say:

I use Bitwarden as my password manager. Emergency access is set up for my spouse. Instructions for requesting access are in the estate folder. A sealed recovery envelope is stored in the home safe. Do not open the envelope unless I have died or become permanently incapacitated.

Or:

I use 1Password. My executor does not need direct access to most accounts. Financial accounts should be handled through the providers using estate documents. Password manager access should only be used to locate subscriptions, cloud files, domain registrations and personal records after legal authority has been confirmed.

Or:

My passwords are stored in Apple iCloud Keychain. The most important accounts are listed in the digital estate inventory. Recovery depends on access to my phone, Apple ID and trusted device. Device recovery instructions are stored with the estate documents.

The exact wording matters less than the principle. The plan should describe the system clearly enough that someone competent can follow it, without exposing every credential unnecessarily.

You should also decide which accounts should not be accessed through passwords at all.

Financial accounts are the obvious example. Even if your password manager contains a bank login, that does not mean your family should log in after your death. Banks, brokers, pension funds, retirement accounts and insurance companies usually have formal processes for dealing with deceased customers. Those processes may be slower, but they exist for a reason. Your inventory should help your family identify the institution and account type, then contact the provider properly.

The same may apply to business systems, medical records, government accounts and certain professional platforms. Access may require legal authority, not just technical ability.

This is why the credential access plan should not be treated as permission to open everything. It is a recovery tool, not a shortcut around the law.

A useful plan can include a simple instruction: when in doubt, contact the provider through the official estate process rather than logging in as me.

That one sentence may prevent mistakes.

Password planning also needs maintenance. If you change password managers, update the plan. If you change your master password, update the backup method. If your nominated emergency contact changes, update the instructions. If you move the sealed envelope, update the location. If you add two-factor authentication, make sure the recovery chain still works.

The good news is that this maintenance does not need to be constant. A well-designed credential access plan may only need an annual review. You are not updating every password. You are checking that the system still works.

Ask yourself:

  • Do I still use the same password manager?
  • Does the emergency access person still make sense?
  • Does that person know the plan exists?
  • Are recovery instructions still stored where the inventory says they are?
  • Have I changed phones, email accounts or two-factor methods?
  • Are any critical accounts outside the password manager?
  • Have I stored anything dangerously in the wrong place?

These questions take minutes. They can save your family hours, days or weeks.

The deeper point is that passwords are not the plan. They are only one part of the access system.

A family dealing with your estate does not need a pile of secrets. They need a controlled way to move from not knowing to knowing, from knowing to locating, and from locating to accessing only where access is appropriate.

That is the difference between leaving behind a keyring and leaving behind instructions. A keyring can be stolen. Instructions can be followed.

The goal is to help your family without making yourself vulnerable.

Chapter 5

The Email Account That Unlocks Everything

Most people think of email as a place where messages arrive. Work updates. Receipts. Newsletters. Appointment reminders. Password resets. Bank alerts. Travel confirmations. Family photos. Old conversations. Documents sent years ago and never filed anywhere else.

That description makes email sound ordinary.

It is not.

Your primary email account may be the most important account in your digital life because it often sits behind everything else. If someone controls your email, they may be able to reset passwords, confirm identity, discover accounts, access records, cancel services, retrieve documents and piece together the map of your life faster than they could from almost anywhere else.

This is why email matters so much in digital estate planning. It is not just another account on the list. It is often the account that reveals the list.

When families are trying to deal with someone's affairs after death, they rarely begin with perfect information. They may not know which bank accounts exist. They may not know which insurance policies are active. They may not know which subscriptions are being charged. They may not know where the person stored photos, tax records, business documents or travel bookings. They may not even know which email address was used for important accounts.

But email often knows.

Search for "policy," and insurance records may appear. Search for "statement," and bank or investment emails may surface. Search for "invoice," and subscriptions, business services and recurring payments become visible. Search for "domain," and website registrations may appear. Search for "Dropbox," "Google Drive," "iCloud," "Super," "pension," "brokerage," "tax," "receipt," "renewal," "flight," "lawyer," "account" or "verification," and suddenly the fog begins to lift.

That makes email useful. It also makes it dangerous.

An email inbox is not a filing cabinet with neat folders labelled "financial," "family," "legal" and "private." It is a living archive of ordinary life. It contains administrative records, but it may also contain private conversations, old arguments, medical information, business matters, personal notes, photos, drafts, regrets, jokes, searches, purchases and messages never intended for anyone else.

This is the tension.

Your family may one day need information from your email. That does not mean they need unrestricted access to your entire inner life.

A good digital estate plan has to deal with both realities.

The first step is to stop treating all email accounts as equal. Most people have more than one. There may be a current personal email address, an older address from years ago, a work email, a business email, a Gmail account used for Android or YouTube, an Apple ID email, an Outlook account, an address used for online shopping, and perhaps one or two forgotten addresses that still control old accounts.

Some of these accounts matter deeply. Others barely matter at all.

Your job is not to document every email you have ever created with the same level of detail. Your job is to identify which email accounts act as gateways.

A gateway email account is one that can unlock, reveal or control other parts of your digital life. Your main personal email is usually one. Your Apple ID or Google account may be another. A business email may be critical if it controls domains, client records, cloud storage, accounting software or payment platforms. An old email may matter if it is still attached to a bank, investment account, pension account, social media profile or cloud service.

This is where many digital estate plans fail. They list the obvious current email account and forget the old one that still controls something important.

The old email address is often the weak link.

Maybe you created it fifteen years ago. Maybe it uses a password you reused everywhere. Maybe it has no two-factor authentication. Maybe it is connected to an old phone number. Maybe you rarely check it. But if it remains the recovery email for a financial account, social media profile, online storage service or domain registration, it still matters.

An account you no longer use can still control an account you still need, which is why an email review is worth doing slowly.

Begin with your current primary email account and ask what it controls. Which bank accounts send alerts there? Which investment platforms use it? Which insurance providers? Which government services? Which subscriptions? Which cloud storage accounts? Which app stores? Which password manager? Which domain registrar? Which business tools?

Then look at your older accounts. Search your password manager, browser-saved passwords, phone mail app, contact records and old devices. You may find addresses you have not thought about in years. Do not assume they are irrelevant because you do not use them daily. Their importance depends on what they still control.

There are three different questions to answer about each email account.

First, can someone find it?

Second, should someone access it?

Third, if access is appropriate, how should that happen?

Those questions are not the same.

Finding the account may simply mean knowing that it exists, what provider hosts it, and what it is used for. Access may require legal authority, device access, recovery codes, two-factor authentication or provider-specific processes. Action may mean preserving documents, closing the account, transferring business records, downloading photos, or leaving private material alone.

A useful email plan does not say, "Here is my inbox. Read everything."

It says, "Here are the email accounts that matter, here is what each one is used for, here is where formal access instructions are stored, and here is what I do and do not want done with them."

This is especially important for your primary email.

Your primary email may be needed for practical reasons. It may contain bills, insurance notices, tax records, account alerts, funeral information, travel bookings, subscription receipts, business correspondence, domain renewals, family documents and contact details. It may help your executor identify providers that need to be contacted. It may also help your family recover photos, personal writing or records that would otherwise be lost.

But the same inbox may also contain personal conversations. That does not make the account impossible to include in your plan. It simply means your instructions should be specific.

For example, you might say that your email should be searched only for estate administration, financial records, business records, subscriptions, photos and family documents. You might say certain folders should not be opened. You might say personal messages should be deleted unread. You might say a particular person is allowed to recover photos, while another person handles administrative records. You might say your executor should access the account only after legal authority has been confirmed.

These instructions will not remove every awkward judgement call, but they will reduce the amount of guessing.

Silence is rarely privacy. Silence forces people to improvise.

A clear instruction gives them a boundary.

This matters because the person handling your affairs may not know what is private, what is important and what is both. Without guidance, they may feel obligated to search everything. They may read more than they wanted to read because they are afraid of missing something important. They may preserve material that you would have preferred to delete. They may close an account before recovering documents or photos. They may leave an account open because they are unsure what to do.

Planning is not just about access. It is about judgement.

You can help by creating a simple email map.

An email map is not a password list. It is a short guide to your email accounts and their purpose. It should include the email address, provider, main use, importance level, recovery method and suggested action. It should not include live passwords unless you have chosen a secure method for storing them separately.

For example:

Personal Gmail account. Used for family, banking alerts, subscriptions, photos and most online accounts. High importance. Credentials stored in password manager. Emergency access instructions in estate folder. Search for financial records, subscriptions, insurance, photos and family documents. Personal folders marked private should not be reviewed unless legally necessary.

Apple ID email. Used for iCloud, app purchases, device backups and photos. High importance. Access may require iPhone passcode and Apple account recovery process. Preserve photos before closing anything.

Old Outlook account. Used mainly for older subscriptions and a few legacy logins. Medium importance. Check for active accounts, then close if no longer needed.

Business email. Used for website, domain, client files and business tools. High importance. Follow business continuity instructions before closing. Contact accountant and nominated business contact first.

That level of detail is enough to be useful without becoming reckless.

You are not trying to hand someone a live wire. You are giving them a labelled switchboard.

The next issue is recovery.

Email access is rarely controlled by the password alone. Most important email accounts now involve two-factor authentication, backup codes, recovery email addresses, trusted devices, phone numbers or security prompts. This is good security while you are alive, but it can create serious problems after death or incapacity.

If your family has the email password but not the phone, they may still be locked out. If they have the phone but not the passcode, they may still be locked out. If the recovery phone number belongs to a disconnected mobile service, they may be blocked. If the recovery email is an old account nobody knows about, they may hit a wall. If backup codes exist but nobody knows where they are, the plan fails at the final step.

This is why email planning cannot be separated from device planning. The two are connected.

For now, focus on the recovery chain. For each important email account, ask:

  • What password manager stores the credential?
  • What device receives the two-factor code?
  • What phone number is attached?
  • What recovery email is listed?
  • Are backup codes available?
  • Where are recovery instructions stored?
  • Who is authorised to begin recovery?

If you cannot answer those questions, your family probably cannot either.

You do not need to write every sensitive detail in the email map. In many cases, you should not. But you should document where the secure recovery instructions live. The map might say, "Recovery codes are stored in the password manager," or "Account recovery instructions are in the sealed envelope in the estate folder," or "Access depends on the iPhone passcode, which is handled in the device instructions."

This keeps the map useful without making it dangerous.

Another useful step is separating email by function while you are alive. This is not essential, but it can make estate planning easier. Some people use one email for everything. That works, but it creates a messy inbox. Others use separate addresses for personal life, finance, business, newsletters and shopping. This can make important records easier to find and private material easier to protect.

You do not need to rebuild your digital life overnight. But you can improve the structure gradually.

If your main inbox is chaotic, start by identifying important categories. Create folders or labels for finance, insurance, tax, estate, property, business, medical, subscriptions and family records. You do not need to organise every old message. Focus on the records someone may actually need. If a provider sends important documents by email, make it easier to find them later.

You can also create a simple folder called "Estate Admin" or "Important Records" and move copies of key emails there. Be careful with sensitive information, but do not underestimate how helpful this can be. A funeral plan, insurance confirmation, solicitor email, tax document, domain renewal or pension statement buried in an inbox of 80,000 messages is technically available but practically hidden.

A folder is not a plan. But it can support the plan.

You should also consider what happens to email accounts after the estate is settled.

Some accounts should be preserved for a period. Some should be closed. Some may need to remain open while subscriptions, financial matters, tax obligations, business responsibilities or family records are handled. Some may be memorialised or deleted depending on provider policies and your wishes.

Do not assume every email account should be closed immediately. Closing an account too quickly can cause problems. Password resets may stop working. Records may disappear. Linked accounts may become harder to recover. Photos or documents may be lost. Business services may fail. Domain renewals may be missed.

At the same time, leaving accounts open indefinitely is not ideal either. Dormant accounts can become security risks. They can be compromised, used for fraud or targeted because nobody is monitoring them. They may continue receiving sensitive information long after anyone is paying attention.

The better approach is staged.

First, identify and preserve what matters.

Second, use the account to locate providers, documents and obligations.

Third, transfer or close linked services where appropriate.

Fourth, close, memorialise or archive the email account when it is no longer needed.

Your plan can say this plainly. "Do not close my primary email account until financial accounts, subscriptions, cloud storage, photos and business records have been reviewed." That single instruction may prevent a lot of damage.

There is also the question of work email.

A work email account is not usually yours in the same way a personal email account is yours. It belongs to the employer or organisation. Your family may have no right to access it, and they may not need to. If important personal documents are stored only in a work email account, move them somewhere appropriate while you are alive. Do not leave your family dependent on an employer granting access to a mailbox they may be legally required to protect.

The same applies if you run a business. A business email account may contain client records, supplier agreements, invoices, staff information, contracts, tax records, domain details and software access. That account may be essential to continuity. It should be included in your plan, but with care. Business access should be handled in line with legal ownership, privacy obligations and any succession plan.

If you are a sole trader, creator, consultant or small business owner, your email may be the operating system of the business. It may hold every client relationship, quote, invoice, login, renewal and supplier thread. If you disappear, the business may become almost impossible to understand without it.

That does not mean someone should casually read everything. It means you need clearer instructions.

Who should contact clients?

Who should access invoices?

Who should notify suppliers?

Where are contracts stored?

Which accounts depend on this email?

What should happen to the domain?

What should happen to the website?

Who can make decisions?

These questions belong in your business continuity notes, but email is often where the answers begin.

One more thing deserves attention: email addresses used as identity.

For many online services, your email address is not just a communication channel. It is your username. It is your login identity. If the email account disappears, the identity can become difficult to prove. This is another reason not to close important email accounts too quickly.

Your family may need time.

The estate may need time.

Systems certainly need time.

A digital estate plan should not rush people into permanent decisions during the most confused period.

If you only do one thing after reading this chapter, do this: identify your primary email account and write down what it controls. Not the password. Not every message. Just what it controls.

That exercise alone can change how you see your digital life.

You may realise one email address sits behind nearly everything. You may realise an old address still matters. You may realise your recovery phone number is outdated. You may realise your password manager, Apple ID, bank alerts and investment accounts all depend on the same inbox. You may realise your family would have no idea where to begin.

That is not a failure. It is the point of the exercise.

You cannot plan for what you cannot see.

Email is where a lot of the digital estate becomes visible.

Treat it accordingly.

Chapter 6

Phones, Devices and Two-Factor Authentication

For most of human history, the important things people left behind were visible. A house. A car. A box of papers. A filing cabinet. A jewellery drawer. A wallet. A set of keys. Even when families did not know exactly where everything was, they could usually search the physical world and find clues.

Modern life is different.

A phone can now hold more practical authority than a filing cabinet ever did. It may contain banking apps, payment cards, email accounts, family photos, authentication codes, travel documents, business messages, investment platforms, cloud storage, medical records, notes, contacts, calendars, passwords and the apps that control almost everything else.

It looks like a device.

In an estate, it behaves more like a gate.

This is why phones and computers deserve their own chapter. They are not just places where information is stored. They are often the tools required to prove identity, receive codes, unlock accounts and complete recovery processes. Your family may know which email address matters. They may know which password manager you use. They may even have legal authority to handle your affairs. But if the final confirmation code is sent to a locked phone nobody can open, the process can stop immediately.

Security has improved, but it has also become more personal. Passwords used to be the main barrier. Now access often depends on a combination of something you know, something you have and something you are. A password. A phone. A fingerprint. A face. A recovery code. A trusted device. A backup email. A SIM card. A passcode.

That is excellent when you are alive and trying to stop strangers from getting in. It can be brutally inconvenient when the person who needs to get in is your executor, spouse, adult child or business partner.

This is one of the central tensions of digital estate planning. Strong security protects you from theft. Strong security without recovery planning can also protect your digital life from the people you meant to help.

The goal is not to weaken your security. It is to make recovery possible for the right person, at the right time, under the right conditions. The phone is usually the place to begin.

For many people, the phone is the most important device they own. It receives text messages. It approves bank logins. It holds authenticator apps. It stores photos. It unlocks email. It controls app stores. It contains contact lists, calendars, notes and messages. It may hold digital wallets, ride-share accounts, airline apps, health records and two-factor authentication prompts.

If someone can open your phone, they may be able to understand a great deal of your life. If nobody can open it, much of your life may become harder to administer.

Phone access is more complicated than people realise because there are several layers involved. There is the device passcode. There may be biometric access, such as Face ID or fingerprint recognition. There is the Apple ID or Google account attached to the phone. There is the mobile number connected to the SIM or eSIM. There may be an authenticator app. There may be cloud backups. There may be device encryption. There may be apps that require their own passwords even after the phone is unlocked.

A family member might know your phone passcode but not your Apple ID password. They might have your Apple ID password but not the device passcode. They might have both, but not be able to receive SMS codes because the mobile service has been cancelled. They might receive codes but be unable to open the authenticator app. They might access the phone but discover that photos are stored in the cloud under a different account.

This is why "they can just use my phone" is not a plan. It is a hope.

A device plan should answer a few basic questions. Which devices matter? Where are they usually kept? What are they used for? Which accounts depend on them? What should happen to them? Where are recovery instructions stored? Who should handle them?

You do not need to write every passcode in a visible document. In fact, you probably should not. But you do need to make clear that certain devices are important and that access instructions exist somewhere secure.

For example, your digital estate inventory might say:

My iPhone is the primary trusted device for Apple ID, banking codes, password manager authentication and family photos. Device access instructions are stored in the sealed envelope in the home safe. Do not erase or reset this phone until photos, authenticator codes and important apps have been reviewed.

That one instruction could prevent a disaster.

Without it, someone trying to be helpful might factory reset the phone, cancel the mobile plan or trade in the device before realising it was the only path into your accounts.

This happens more easily than you think. After a death, families often try to simplify. They cancel services. They close accounts. They sell devices. They return work equipment. They clear drawers. They remove clutter. These actions feel practical, but digital systems often depend on things that look disposable.

An old phone may receive recovery codes. A tablet may still be logged into cloud storage. A laptop may hold tax files. An external drive may contain family photos. A SIM card may be attached to banking verification. A notebook may contain recovery instructions. A USB stick may hold important documents. A device that looks outdated may be the bridge to something current.

Your family does not need to keep every piece of technology forever. They only need to know what not to destroy too quickly.

Two-factor authentication makes this even more important.

Two-factor authentication, often called 2FA or multi-factor authentication, adds a second proof of identity when logging in. Instead of using only a password, you may also need a code from a text message, an authenticator app, a push notification, a hardware security key or a recovery code.

This is good security. It makes stolen passwords less useful. If someone guesses or obtains your password, they still need the second factor.

But estate planning changes the question. If your family has legal authority and the password, can they also obtain the second factor? If not, the account may remain inaccessible.

There are several common types of two-factor authentication.

SMS codes are sent to your phone number. They are convenient, but they depend on the mobile service remaining active. If the phone plan is cancelled too early, the number is transferred, the SIM is lost or the device is locked, SMS recovery may fail.

Authenticator apps generate time-based codes on your phone. Examples include Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, Authy, 1Password, Bitwarden, iCloud Keychain and other password manager-based code systems. These are generally more secure than SMS, but they can create recovery problems if nobody can access the device or if the authenticator app is not backed up.

Push notifications ask you to approve a login on a trusted device. This can be simple while you are alive because the notification appears on your phone. It can be difficult for someone else if the phone is locked, missing or disabled.

Hardware security keys are physical devices, often USB or NFC keys, used to approve access. They can be very secure, but only if the key can be found and the person handling the estate knows what it is for. To an uninformed family member, a security key may look like a random USB stick.

Backup codes are one-time codes generated by many services when you set up two-factor authentication. They are meant to be stored safely and used if the primary second factor is unavailable. They are extremely useful in estate planning, but only if they exist, are current and can be found.

The mistake is not using two-factor authentication. The mistake is using it without a recovery plan.

A good plan records the method, not necessarily the secret.

For each critical account, your inventory might say:

  • two-factor method: authenticator app
  • authenticator stored in: 1Password
  • recovery codes stored in: password manager secure notes
  • trusted device: iPhone
  • emergency instructions: sealed envelope in estate folder

Or:

  • two-factor method: SMS
  • phone number: personal mobile
  • mobile provider: Telstra
  • instruction: do not cancel phone service until account recovery is complete

Or:

  • two-factor method: hardware security key
  • key location: home safe
  • backup key location: solicitor file
  • instruction: do not discard unlabelled USB-style keys

Notice what this does. It gives the person handling your estate enough direction to understand the system without printing sensitive codes in an unsafe place.

That is the balance you are trying to strike.

Devices also raise questions about encryption. Modern phones and laptops are usually encrypted by default. This means the data on the device is protected unless the correct passcode, password or recovery method is used. Encryption is a good thing. It protects photos, documents, messages, financial data and private information if the device is stolen.

But encryption does not care whether the person trying to access the device is a thief or your executor. Without the right access method, the data may be unrecoverable.

This is especially true for phones. A six-digit passcode may look simple, but it can be the difference between recovering family photos and losing them permanently. Biometric access may not work after death. Face ID or fingerprint access should not be treated as a reliable estate access method. The passcode matters.

For computers, the same issue appears with login passwords, disk encryption and cloud accounts. A laptop may contain important files, but if the login password is unknown and the disk is encrypted, recovery may be difficult or impossible. A cloud backup may exist, but it may require the same account credentials and two-factor method that nobody can access.

This is why your plan should identify important devices and explain what they contain.

Not every device needs the same level of planning. A streaming tablet used only for Netflix probably does not matter. A work laptop may belong to your employer and should be returned. A personal laptop containing tax records, business documents, manuscripts, photos and estate files matters much more. An old external hard drive may matter if it contains the only copy of family photos.

The important question is simple: if this device disappeared or stayed locked forever, what would be lost?

If the answer is "nothing important," you do not need much detail.

If the answer is "photos, business records, tax documents, recovery codes or access to other accounts," the device belongs in the plan.

You should also think about cloud backups. Many people assume their photos and files are "on the phone," when they are actually in iCloud, Google Photos, OneDrive, Dropbox or another cloud service. The phone is only one access point. This can be helpful because data may be recoverable even if the device is lost. It can also be confusing because the account, device and cloud service are tied together.

Your plan should say where important files actually live.

Photos: iCloud Photos under Apple ID.

Documents: Google Drive under Gmail account.

Business files: Dropbox under business email.

Tax records: local laptop folder and OneDrive backup.

Manuscripts: external drive and cloud folder.

This matters because families often search the wrong place. They may spend hours trying to unlock a device when the files are safely stored in the cloud. Or they may close a cloud account because they think everything was stored locally. A simple note can prevent both mistakes.

There is another device category people forget: smart home and digital identity devices.

Security cameras, door locks, alarm systems, routers, smart speakers, connected lights, home assistants and home automation apps may all be attached to someone's account. If that person dies or becomes incapacitated, the household may lose control of ordinary things. The family may not know how to change Wi-Fi settings, cancel monitoring, access camera footage, transfer ownership or reset devices.

This is not usually the most urgent part of an estate, but it can become a practical headache at the worst possible time.

The same applies to vehicles with connected apps, toll accounts, digital keys, charging accounts, parking apps and navigation subscriptions. The modern home and car increasingly depend on accounts that someone else may need to manage.

You do not need to document every light bulb. But you should document systems that affect access, security, money or daily function.

Think in categories:

  • phones
  • tablets
  • laptops
  • desktop computers
  • external drives
  • USB sticks
  • hardware security keys
  • smart home systems
  • routers and Wi-Fi
  • vehicles and connected apps
  • business devices
  • work devices
  • old devices that may still hold data

Then decide what matters.

A useful device plan may include:

  • device name
  • approximate location
  • what it is used for
  • whether it contains important files
  • whether it receives authentication codes
  • whether it is backed up
  • whether it should be preserved, transferred, returned, wiped or recycled
  • where access instructions are stored

This sounds like a lot, but it becomes simple once you start. Most people have only a few devices that really matter.

The phone. The laptop. The tablet. The external drive. The old phone in the drawer. The hardware key, if they use one.

Five items may carry most of the risk.

The other issue is timing. In estate administration, timing matters more than people realise. Some actions should happen quickly, such as preserving access to important devices, keeping the phone service active temporarily and locating backup codes. Other actions should wait, such as wiping devices, closing cloud accounts, cancelling numbers or resetting hardware.

Your plan should include a "do not do this yet" list.

For example:

  • do not cancel my mobile phone plan until two-factor authentication has been reviewed
  • do not erase my phone until photos and authenticator apps have been checked
  • do not factory reset my laptop until files have been copied
  • do not throw away old phones, USB drives or security keys
  • do not close cloud storage until important files have been downloaded
  • do not return business devices until business continuity instructions have been reviewed
  • do not reset the home router unless Wi-Fi and smart home accounts have been documented

This kind of list is practical, not dramatic. It helps people avoid permanent mistakes.

It also reduces conflict. If several family members are trying to help, one person may want to clean up quickly while another wants to preserve everything. Clear instructions give everyone a shared reference point.

You should also decide who is technically capable of helping. The person you trust emotionally may not be the best person to handle devices. That does not mean they should be excluded. It means you may want to name a technically confident helper who can assist the executor or family member.

This could be an adult child, sibling, friend, business partner, IT provider or professional adviser. Their role should be limited and clear. They may help locate files, preserve data, export photos, identify accounts or transfer business systems. They should not be given unlimited authority simply because they understand technology.

Trust and technical skill are different qualities. A good plan recognises both.

There is also a privacy layer. Devices often contain the most personal material someone leaves behind. Photos. Messages. Notes. Browsing history. Health data. Private documents. Drafts. Conversations. You may not want all of that opened broadly.

Again, the answer is instruction.

You can say which devices contain personal material. You can say who should recover photos. You can say which folders should be preserved. You can say which folders should be deleted unread. You can say business files should be separated from personal files. You can say old devices should be wiped only after a named person confirms the important data has been copied.

A device plan does not remove grief or judgement. It gives grief something to lean on.

The most useful plans are not long. They are clear.

My iPhone matters because it receives authentication codes and contains family photos.

My laptop matters because it contains tax records, business documents and writing files.

My old phone matters because it may still contain photos from before iCloud backup.

My external drive matters because it contains photo archives.

My hardware security key is in the safe and should not be discarded.

My mobile phone service should stay active for at least three months after death unless the executor confirms it is no longer needed.

That is enough to prevent many of the most common mistakes.

As with passwords and email, the point is not to make access casual. It is to make recovery possible.

If you are worried about security, that worry is healthy. Do not respond by hiding everything. Hidden systems fail families. Instead, separate the sensitive access details from the plain-language map. The map says what matters and where instructions are stored. The secure instructions contain the details needed when the time comes.

The map can be read.

The keys stay protected.

That is the structure you are building across this book.

By now, a pattern should be emerging. You are not trying to create a giant document filled with secrets. You are creating a calm path through a confusing estate. Each chapter adds another part of that path.

A password without the phone may not be enough.

An email address without the recovery code may not be enough.

A cloud account without the trusted device may not be enough.

A laptop without the login password may not be enough.

Planning connects the pieces before someone else has to discover them under pressure.

That is the work.

Chapter 7

Photos, Files and the Things Families Actually Want Back

When people talk about digital estate planning, they usually begin with passwords, bank accounts and legal access. That makes sense. Those are the urgent problems. Bills need to be paid. Accounts need to be identified. Insurance needs to be claimed. Subscriptions need to be stopped. Devices need to be preserved before someone resets them without thinking.

But once the urgent work settles, families often discover that the thing they care about most is not an account at all. It is the photo they cannot find, the video from a birthday, the voice note from a parent, the folder of old travel photos, the recipe typed into a notes app, the half-written family history, the scan of a child's drawing, the document that explains something nobody else remembers, or the message thread that, for reasons no one could have predicted, becomes precious.

These things rarely look important while life is moving. They sit in camera rolls, cloud folders, old laptops, WhatsApp threads, hard drives, email attachments, desktop folders and half-forgotten backups. They are mixed in with screenshots, receipts, memes, duplicates, blurry photos, downloaded PDFs, tax records, work files, invoices and the general sediment of modern life.

Then someone dies, and suddenly the ordinary archive becomes irreplaceable.

This is one of the harder truths about digital life. We create more personal material than any generation before us, but much of it is stored in places nobody else understands. The family photo album used to sit on a shelf. The shoebox of letters could be found in a cupboard. The important documents were often in a filing cabinet, even if the filing system was imperfect. Today, the record of a life may be spread across five devices, three cloud services, two messaging apps, an email inbox, an old hard drive and a phone nobody can unlock.

That does not mean the past was better. Digital storage has given us extraordinary abundance. We can keep thousands of photos, search old documents, preserve videos, back up files and carry decades of memories in a pocket. The problem is not that digital records are inferior. The problem is that they are often invisible to the people who may one day need them.

A good digital estate plan should not only help your family close accounts. It should help them recover what is worth keeping.

That requires a different kind of thinking.

Financial accounts are usually handled through formal processes. Email accounts can be mapped. Passwords can be planned. Devices can be preserved. But photos and personal files are messy because they are not always clearly labelled, legally urgent or neatly separated from private material. They matter because they are human, and human things rarely fit into tidy categories.

The first question is not "Where are all my files?"

That question is too big. Most people have more files than they realise, and many of them do not matter. The better question is: "What would my family be grateful to find?"

That changes the exercise immediately.

They may not need every screenshot. They may not need every work draft. They may not need every downloaded menu, boarding pass or appliance manual. They may not need the 43 nearly identical photos you took of the same sunset. But they may want family photos, videos, important documents, creative work, recipes, letters, audio recordings, personal notes, business records, property documents, tax files, passwords to shared services, and anything that helps them understand your wishes.

The work is not to catalogue everything. It is to protect the meaningful things from being lost inside everything else. Photos are usually the easiest place to begin because their emotional value is obvious.

Photos are usually the most emotionally valuable digital asset people leave behind. They are also one of the easiest to lose through confusion. Many families assume photos are stored on the phone. Sometimes they are. Sometimes they are mostly in iCloud. Sometimes they are in Google Photos. Sometimes they are split between an old laptop, a current phone, WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, Dropbox, OneDrive, an external drive and a folder called "Photos backup final final."

The danger is not only that photos disappear. It is that nobody knows where the complete collection lives.

A person may open an old laptop, find a few hundred photos and assume that is the archive. They may not realise the last ten years are in iCloud. Or they may preserve the phone but cancel the cloud subscription attached to the account. They may download low-resolution copies from social media while the original files sit in a cloud folder they never knew existed.

A simple photo map can prevent this.

You do not need to organise every image. You need to document where the main photo collections are stored and what should happen to them. For example: "Family photos are mostly in iCloud Photos under my Apple ID. Older photos from 2005 to 2016 are on the external hard drive labelled Family Archive. WhatsApp contains some family videos that are not backed up elsewhere. Please download the iCloud library before closing the account."

It is not fancy, but it is useful.

The same principle applies to videos. Videos are easy to overlook because they are scattered across camera rolls, messaging apps, social media, cloud folders and old devices. They also take more storage, which makes people more likely to delete them casually. A short video of someone laughing at a dinner table may matter more to a family than a thousand formal photos. If there are videos you would want preserved, say where they are.

Voice recordings deserve special attention too. Many people have audio files they barely think about: voice memos, recorded interviews, WhatsApp voice notes, old voicemails, podcast drafts, business calls, family messages or recordings of children. Some will be irrelevant. Some may be deeply meaningful. The difficulty is that families may not know to look for them.

If you have recordings that matter, mention them.

The next category is documents. These are less emotional but often more urgent. Tax returns. Insurance policies. Property records. Business registrations. Contracts. Wills and estate documents. Loan documents. Trust documents. Vehicle records. Medical documents. Qualifications. Immigration records. Legal correspondence. Receipts for major purchases. Manuals for important systems. Letters from advisers.

Documents have a habit of hiding in too many places. Some live in email. Some are in cloud storage. Some are scanned into notes apps. Some are saved on a desktop. Some are in accounting software. Some are downloaded but never moved from the downloads folder. Some are printed. Some exist only as attachments in old messages.

Again, the answer is not to create a perfect archive overnight. It is to identify where the important documents are most likely to be found and make that location clear.

A useful document map might say: "Tax records are stored in Google Drive under Finance / Tax. Insurance documents are in the Insurance folder. Property documents are in Dropbox under Property. Business contracts are in Xero and in the business email account. Estate documents are printed in the estate folder, with digital copies in the Legal folder."

This sort of instruction may feel obvious to you. It will not be obvious to someone else, especially under pressure.

There is another reason to create a cleaner document structure while you are alive: it helps you too. A digital estate plan should not only be useful after death. It should make your current life easier. If you cannot find your own insurance policy, tax records or property documents quickly, your family will struggle even more.

Estate planning often reveals operational problems in your personal life. That is not a criticism. It is an opportunity.

The third category is personal writing and creative work. This includes manuscripts, journals, notes, essays, drafts, business ideas, family history, recipes, letters, designs, music, photos, videos, artwork, research and anything you have made that may matter to someone later.

Creative work is often vulnerable because it may not look official. A folder of drafts may not seem important to a family member. A notes app may look like clutter. A hard drive may be wiped because nobody knows it contains years of writing. A cloud folder may expire because no one realises it holds the only copy of a project.

If you create things, document where they live.

You do not need to overstate their importance. You simply need to give someone enough context to avoid destroying them accidentally. "My writing is stored in iCloud Drive under Writing. Draft books are in the Manuscripts folder. Please preserve these files and share them with [person]." That sentence can protect years of work.

This matters even if you are not a professional writer, artist or creator. Personal writing can become meaningful. A journal, family story, recipe collection, letter to children, travel diary, sermon, speech, notebook or memoir draft may have no commercial value and enormous family value.

Do not let "not published" become "not worth preserving."

The fourth category is business and operational files. If you own or operate a business, your files may be needed for continuity. Staff records, contractor agreements, supplier lists, insurance documents, invoices, bank records, policies, operating procedures, templates, client lists, domain details, brand files, licences, reports, passwords, asset registers and accounting records may all sit across different systems.

Business files need a different standard because other people may depend on them. Family photos can wait a few weeks. Payroll may not. Supplier payments may not. Client obligations may not. Lease terms may not. Tax deadlines may not.

If your business relies on your files, your plan should identify the critical systems and the person who should handle them. This does not mean giving your family free access to everything. It means making sure the right person can keep the business stable or wind it down properly.

A business file map should answer: where are the records, who understands them, what needs attention quickly, and what should not be touched without advice?

That last part matters. Sometimes a well-meaning family member can cause damage by trying to "sort things out" without understanding the business. Clear instructions can prevent that. "Contact the accountant before closing any business account." "Do not cancel the domain or email hosting." "Client files are confidential and should be handled by [person]." "Supplier agreements are stored in the Contracts folder."

These are small instructions, but they create order.

The fifth category is sentimental digital material that does not fit neatly anywhere else. Message threads. Social media posts. Shared albums. Group chats. Online communities. Playlists. Digital art. Game accounts. Family calendars. Notes. Personal lists. Recipes. Saved articles. Book highlights. Screenshots of conversations. Emails from people who are gone.

Some of this may not matter. Some of it may matter more than you expect.

The challenge is deciding what should be preserved without forcing your family to inspect everything. One way to do this is to create a small "Keep" folder while you are alive. This could be a folder in cloud storage called Family Archive, Personal Archive, Important Memories or something similar. Inside it, place copies of files you would want someone to find: meaningful photos, letters, recipes, scans, videos, audio, documents or notes.

It does not need to be comprehensive. It needs to be intentional.

Think of it as the modern version of the shoebox.

The shoebox did not contain every record of a life. It contained the things someone chose to keep.

A digital "Keep" folder works the same way.

It also helps with privacy. If you create a folder of material you are comfortable sharing, your family does not have to search as widely. They may still need to handle administrative accounts, but they will have a clear place to begin for personal material. That reduces the chance they will wander through private folders looking for memories.

This is one of the best reasons to plan. It gives people permission not to search everything.

Another useful habit is naming folders plainly. Clever folder names are enjoyable while you are alive and annoying when someone else needs to understand them. A folder called "Old Stuff" may contain nothing or everything. A folder called "Tax 2025" is better. A folder called "Family Photos 2010 to 2020" is better. A folder called "Estate Documents" is better. A folder called "Writing Manuscripts" is better.

Plain names are a gift.

You should also be careful with duplicates and backups. Many people have several versions of the same archive. An old laptop backup, an external hard drive backup, an iCloud library, a Google Photos export, a Dropbox folder and a folder on a desktop. Duplicates are not the enemy, but confusion is. If there is one main archive, say so. If there are older backups, label them as older backups. If a drive should not be trusted as complete, note that.

Your family does not need to know every technical detail. They need to know which copy matters most.

File formats can create another problem. Some files may be stored in formats that become hard to open. Old videos, design files, accounting backups, databases, encrypted archives or proprietary software files may need specific apps. If you have important files in unusual formats, include a note. "These files require [software]." "The accounting backup should be opened by the accountant." "The old video files may need conversion."

Again, you are not solving every future technical issue. You are leaving clues.

Backups are part of this too. A file that exists in only one place is fragile. A phone can be lost. A laptop can fail. A hard drive can die. A cloud account can be closed. A password can be forgotten. A subscription can lapse. A family member can accidentally delete something.

The classic backup idea is simple: important files should exist in more than one place. For ordinary people, that may mean cloud storage plus an external drive. For business owners, it may mean cloud storage, local backups and professional systems. For photos, it may mean iCloud or Google Photos plus periodic exports. For estate documents, it may mean printed copies plus digital copies.

The exact system matters less than the principle. If losing one device or account would permanently erase something important, you have a weakness.

This does not mean you need to become a backup expert. It means you should identify your irreplaceable files and make sure they are not dependent on one fragile point.

Irreplaceable is the key word.

You can download another bank statement. You can replace an appliance manual. You can recreate a spreadsheet if you have the data. You cannot recreate a photo from a childhood birthday, a recording of someone's voice, a personal letter, a manuscript that was never backed up, or a folder of family history if the only copy disappears.

Digital estate planning is partly about knowing the difference between recoverable and irreplaceable.

That difference should guide your effort.

You do not need to preserve everything. In fact, trying to preserve everything can make the important things harder to find. The aim is not digital hoarding. The aim is thoughtful preservation.

This brings us back to privacy. There may be files you do not want preserved. Old drafts. Personal notes. Private journals. Sensitive documents. Medical information. Messages. Photos. Work files. Anything that belongs to someone else. A plan can include deletion instructions, but they should be realistic. Once you are gone, someone may need legal authority, technical access and judgement to carry them out.

If privacy is important to you, organise while you are alive. Separate what should be kept from what should not. Do not leave everything mixed together and hope people will know where not to look. They will not know, and they may be afraid to delete anything.

A folder called "Private - Delete Unread" may not be perfect, but it is clearer than silence. Better still, regularly delete what you do not want kept. Estate planning is not only about leaving things behind. Sometimes it is about not leaving unnecessary things behind.

There is a kindness in reducing the burden.

Your family should not have to become archaeologists of your digital clutter. They should not have to open every folder, every inbox, every backup and every device just to find the few things that matter. The more intentional you are now, the less emotional labour you leave for them later.

The best digital file plan is not complicated. It answers five questions:

  • Where are the important photos?
  • Where are the important documents?
  • Where are the personal or creative files worth preserving?
  • Where are the business files, if any?
  • What should be deleted, closed or left alone?

If your plan answers those questions clearly, it will help.

It may help more than you realise.

Because after the forms are filed, the subscriptions cancelled and the accounts closed, what remains are the human things. The photos. The words. The voice. The records that tell people who you were and how you lived. Those things deserve more than being trapped behind a forgotten password or buried in a folder nobody can identify.

A digital estate is not only about money and access. It is also about memory.

Chapter 8

Money, Subscriptions and the Accounts That Keep Charging

Money is usually the part of an estate people understand in theory and struggle with in practice. Everyone knows bank accounts, credit cards, loans, insurance policies and investments matter. What is less obvious is how much of modern financial life is now discovered indirectly, through apps, email receipts, online portals, automatic payments and subscriptions quietly renewing in the background.

A generation ago, a family could often learn a lot by opening the mail. Bank statements arrived in envelopes. Insurance renewals arrived in envelopes. Bills arrived in envelopes. Superannuation, pensions, loan notices, share registries, electricity providers, phone companies and government agencies all left paper trails. The filing system may have been messy, but the clues were physical.

Today the clues are often digital. A bank statement may never be printed. An investment platform may send only an email notification. An insurance policy may live inside an online portal. A subscription may be charged to a card every month without a paper invoice ever touching the house. A business tool may renew automatically once a year. A domain name may be connected to a credit card nobody else knows about. A cloud storage account may contain family photos, but if the payment fails, the account may eventually be downgraded, locked or deleted.

This is where digital estate planning becomes practical very quickly. It is not only about preserving memories or protecting privacy. It is also about stopping avoidable financial confusion.

The problem is that financial accounts and recurring payments often live in different worlds. A family may eventually find the main bank account, but that does not tell them every account connected to it. They may see card charges but not know which services they relate to. They may identify a subscription but not know whether it should be cancelled, transferred, preserved temporarily or left alone until something else is recovered.

Some subscriptions are harmless. Some are annoying. Some are expensive. Some are the only thing keeping an important digital asset alive.

A streaming service can probably be cancelled without much consequence. A cloud storage subscription may need to stay active until photos and documents are downloaded. A password manager renewal may be critical. A domain registration may protect a business, personal brand, family email address or published work. Accounting software may contain tax records. A website hosting account may keep a business online. A paid email account may control access to other services.

The danger is treating every recurring payment as clutter.

When someone dies, families understandably want to reduce the noise. They cancel cards, close accounts, stop direct debits and shut down services. Often that is the right instinct. But with digital accounts, closing the payment source can create a chain reaction. Services may fail. Access may be lost. Data may be deleted. Domains may expire. Email accounts may stop working. Backup systems may stop running. Records may become harder to recover.

The better approach is not to keep everything forever. It is to know what each financial account or subscription supports before making permanent decisions.

Begin with the money accounts: bank accounts, credit cards, loans, mortgages, investment platforms, retirement accounts, superannuation or pension accounts, insurance policies, tax accounts, digital wallets, payment apps and any business financial systems. Some of these will be handled through formal estate procedures, not passwords. That distinction matters. Your family may need to know the provider exists and which account type it is, but they may still need to contact the institution properly with death certificates, estate documents and legal authority.

Your inventory should not encourage people to log into financial accounts as if they were you. In many cases, that may breach terms, create legal issues or complicate the estate process. The purpose of the digital estate plan is to help your executor identify institutions and accounts, not to bypass the proper process.

A useful financial inventory records the provider, account type, approximate purpose, whether statements arrive digitally, which email address is used, and what action should be taken. It does not need to record balances. Balances change constantly and will be confirmed by the provider. What matters is that the account can be found.

For example, the inventory might say: "Personal transaction account with Commonwealth Bank. Statements are electronic. Account notifications go to my main Gmail address. Contact the bank through the deceased estate process." Another line might say: "Share trading account with [provider]. Long-term investment portfolio. Tax statements are issued annually through the online portal. Contact provider through formal estate process."

That level of detail can save days of searching without creating a risky document full of sensitive financial information.

Credit cards need special attention because many recurring payments attach themselves to cards and keep going until the card is cancelled or the service is stopped. A credit card statement can become a subscription map, but only if someone knows to read it that way. The charge names may be cryptic. The amount may vary. Annual renewals may appear only once a year. Some services bill through Apple, Google, PayPal, Stripe or marketplace platforms, which can hide the actual provider.

One practical step is to review your last three months of bank and card statements and list every recurring payment. Then review the last twelve months for annual renewals. Many important services only charge once a year, which means a short review can miss them.

This is not exciting work, but it is revealing. You may find software you forgot you paid for. You may find old subscriptions that should have been cancelled years ago. You may find insurance premiums, storage plans, memberships, domain renewals, app subscriptions, professional tools, cloud services, accounting software, password managers, backup systems, website hosting, charity donations and investment platform fees.

You may also find things you do not want your family to discover by accident. That is another reason to review them now.

Subscriptions are not only a money issue. They are a dependency issue. The right question is not simply, "What am I paying for?" It is, "What breaks if this stops?"

If a music subscription stops, probably not much. If a password manager subscription stops, access planning may become harder. If cloud storage stops, photos and files may be at risk. If domain registration stops, a website or email address may fail. If accounting software stops, business records may become harder to access. If a phone plan stops, two-factor authentication may break. If a hosting account stops, a business may disappear from the internet.

Not all of these services must continue forever, but your plan should tell people which ones deserve caution.

A simple way to categorise subscriptions is: cancel, preserve temporarily, transfer, review, or critical.

Cancel means the service can be stopped without much consequence. Preserve temporarily means it should stay active until files, photos, records or access dependencies have been reviewed. Transfer means ownership or billing should move to another person. Review means someone should understand the service before deciding. Critical means the service supports something important and should not be interrupted casually.

This is much more useful than a raw list of charges.

When Sofia sees a charge for cloud storage, it looks at first like another monthly fee. Without instructions, she may cancel it during the cleanup. With one note, she knows it contains family photos and should stay active until the archive is downloaded. The decision changes because the context changes.

Business owners need an even sharper version of this. Many small businesses now run on quiet subscriptions: accounting software, payroll systems, point-of-sale tools, booking platforms, website hosting, domain names, email hosting, design tools, inventory systems, project management software, customer databases, social media schedulers, cloud storage, payment processors and industry-specific platforms. If the owner dies or becomes incapacitated, these subscriptions may be the rails the business runs on.

The people left behind may not understand which tools are essential and which are optional. They may see a long list of charges and start cancelling to reduce costs. That might be sensible in some cases, but dangerous in others. Payroll, customer bookings, payment processing, email, websites and accounting records should not be treated the same way as a stock photo subscription or a forgotten app trial.

If you operate a business, your plan should identify critical operating subscriptions separately from personal subscriptions. It should say which services must remain active for continuity, who understands them, who should be contacted, and what should happen if the business is sold, closed or transferred.

You do not need to write a full business continuity plan inside your personal estate inventory, but you should create enough direction that someone does not accidentally pull the plug on the wrong system.

Digital wallets and payment apps add another layer. Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal, Wise, Stripe, Square, Afterpay, PayPal subscriptions, app store billing, marketplace accounts and crypto exchanges can all create financial trails that do not look like traditional bank accounts. Some hold balances. Some merely process payments. Some connect to cards. Some connect to business revenue. Some contain records needed for tax.

These should be included in the inventory, especially if money may sit inside them.

Payment processors are particularly important for business owners and creators. A Stripe account, PayPal business account, Shopify Payments account, Amazon KDP account or marketplace seller account may continue receiving revenue after death. Royalties, digital product sales, affiliate payments, subscriptions, invoices and customer payments may keep flowing. If nobody knows the account exists, revenue may sit unclaimed or the business may keep operating without oversight.

This applies to creators as well as traditional businesses. If you publish books, sell templates, run a website, earn advertising revenue, receive affiliate income or operate paid communities, those income streams are part of the estate. They may not feel like "assets" in the traditional sense, but they can have real value.

A digital estate plan should identify where money comes from, not only where money sits.

That distinction matters. A bank account is where money sits. A royalty account, payment processor, marketplace profile or subscription platform is where money may continue to arrive.

If your family only finds the bank account, they may miss the source.

Insurance also deserves its own note. Many people know they have insurance but do not make it easy for family to find the policy. Life insurance, income protection, trauma cover, health insurance, home and contents, landlord insurance, car insurance, business insurance and professional indemnity may all be managed digitally. Premiums may be charged automatically. Policy documents may sit in a portal. Renewal notices may go to email.

Your plan should include the insurer, policy type, policy number if you have it, where documents are stored and who should be contacted. This is one area where a little detail can make an enormous difference. Families should not have to guess whether a policy exists.

The same applies to retirement and superannuation accounts. Depending on where you live, these may not automatically form part of the estate in the same way as other assets, and beneficiary nominations may matter. The practical point is simple: make sure your family knows which providers exist and where the paperwork is. Formal decisions should be made with appropriate legal or financial advice.

Tax records are another area where digital access matters. Your family or executor may need prior-year returns, accountant details, business records, capital gains information, investment statements, invoices, receipts and access to government tax portals. Some of this may be handled by an accountant. Some may be stored in accounting software. Some may be in email. Some may be in cloud folders.

A good plan simply points to the source: "My accountant is [name]. Tax records are in [location]. Investment tax statements are available through [platform]. Business records are in [software]."

This is not only useful after death. It is useful if you are hospitalised, travelling, incapacitated or unable to manage your affairs for a period. Estate planning often sounds final, but many of these instructions are just as valuable during a temporary crisis.

Now return to subscriptions, because this is where many people can make immediate progress. Open your bank statement. Open your credit card statement. Open Apple subscriptions, Google subscriptions, PayPal automatic payments and any business card statements. Then list the recurring charges. Do not try to fix everything at once. Just write them down.

For each one, ask:

  • What is this?
  • Is it personal or business?
  • Is it still needed?
  • Does it store important data?
  • Does it support another account?
  • Should it be cancelled, preserved, transferred or reviewed?
  • Which email address controls it?
  • How often does it renew?

This review will probably save you money while you are alive and save your family confusion later. That is a rare estate planning task with an immediate reward.

You may also find subscriptions that should be moved. If the family cloud storage account is paid from your personal card, should someone else be able to manage it? If the phone plan is in your name but used for household two-factor authentication, does your partner know how to keep it active? If a domain renewal protects a business email address, is the business card paying for it? If a family streaming or storage plan is attached to an email only you can access, should that be documented?

The goal is not to make every account joint or shared. The goal is to notice hidden dependencies.

Some accounts should remain private. Some should be transferred. Some should be left as they are but documented. The right answer depends on the account.

There is one more uncomfortable issue: debt.

Digital estate planning should include debts and obligations, not just assets. Credit cards, personal loans, buy-now-pay-later accounts, tax debts, business debts, vehicle loans, mortgages, store cards, overdrafts and informal repayment arrangements may all have digital records. Some may be obvious. Others may be hidden in apps or email.

Your family does not need a confession document. They need enough information to avoid being blindsided. If there are debts, list the provider and account type. Formal balances can be confirmed later. Hiding obligations rarely protects anyone. It usually makes the estate harder to settle.

The same applies to ongoing commitments. Leases, memberships, contracts, software agreements, service contracts, maintenance agreements, business obligations, sponsorships, professional memberships and annual renewals may all create financial or legal responsibilities. If they exist only in email or a portal, they belong in the plan.

A financial digital estate map is not about control from beyond the grave. It is about reducing confusion. It tells the people handling your affairs where money sits, where money comes from, where money leaves, and which payments should not be interrupted too quickly.

That map should be simple enough to maintain.

A bloated financial inventory will become outdated. A lean one will survive. Record the provider, purpose, account type, email used, where records live and suggested action. Avoid chasing perfect balances. Avoid listing every transaction. Avoid turning the document into a second accounting system.

Your plan should help someone begin.

It does not need to finish the job for them.

The work of estate administration will still require providers, documents, legal authority and judgement. Your plan simply reduces the amount of blind searching required before that work can start.

Money has always left trails. The trails are just less visible now.

Your job is to make them visible enough.

Chapter 9

Social Media, Online Identity and Public Profiles

Social media is one of the strangest parts of a digital estate because it sits between the personal and the public. A bank account is private. A photo folder is usually private. A password manager is private. But a social profile is different. It may contain private messages, family photos, public posts, business contacts, old opinions, memories, creative work, professional history and years of small traces that other people can still see.

That visibility changes the problem.

When someone dies, their social accounts do not automatically feel like ordinary assets. They feel like extensions of the person. A Facebook profile may become the place where friends leave messages. An Instagram account may hold years of family photos. A LinkedIn profile may remain visible to professional contacts. A YouTube channel may continue receiving views. An X account may still be searchable. A website may continue to present someone's work long after they are gone.

For families, this can be comforting, awkward, painful or confusing. Sometimes it is all of those at once.

The practical question is simple: what should happen to your public digital identity when you are no longer here to manage it?

Most people never answer that question. They assume their family will know what feels right. But families often do not know. One person may want to preserve everything. Another may find the reminders painful. One may want to post an announcement. Another may feel that announcing a death online is too public. Friends may keep tagging the person. Birthday reminders may appear. Old posts may resurface. Messages may arrive from people who do not know what happened.

Without instructions, social media can become one more emotional decision at exactly the wrong time.

This chapter is not about vanity, and it is not about controlling how people remember you. It is about reducing uncertainty around accounts that remain visible after death and often carry emotional weight for other people. The useful distinction is between a social account and a social presence.

A social account is the login, platform and technical access. Facebook. Instagram. LinkedIn. X. TikTok. YouTube. Pinterest. Reddit. Threads. Medium. Substack. Discord. WhatsApp groups. Online forums. Gaming profiles. Professional directories. Review sites. Creator platforms. Community memberships.

A social presence is what other people see and experience. Your posts. Your profile photo. Your name. Your bio. Your videos. Your writing. Your comments. Your messages. Your professional history. Your creative archive. Your public reputation. Your old jokes. Your opinions. Your memories.

Estate planning has to deal with both. Your family may need account access or provider support, but they also need to know what you want done with the visible version of you.

Some accounts should be memorialised. Some should be archived. Some should be deleted. Some should be preserved because they contain creative work, business value or family memories. Some may not matter at all. The mistake is treating all public profiles as if they are the same.

A Facebook account used mainly for family photos is different from a LinkedIn profile used for professional identity. A YouTube channel with years of published work is different from an old TikTok account with three posts. A personal Instagram is different from a business Instagram. A Reddit account with private interests is different from a public author profile. A Medium account containing essays is different from a messaging app used for private conversations.

The first step is to list the accounts that represent you publicly. Do not start with every app on your phone. Start with the places where someone could search your name, find your work, contact you, see your photos or assume the account is still active.

This usually includes social media, professional profiles, websites and creator platforms.

Then ask what role each one plays.

Is it personal? Professional? Business-related? Creative? Private? Mostly inactive? Still connected to income? Important to family? Important to customers? Important to your reputation? Important only because it should be closed?

That context matters more than the platform name.

A family handling your estate does not need to know that you have an Instagram account in the abstract. They need to know whether it should be preserved, memorialised, downloaded, transferred, deleted or ignored.

Many platforms have their own policies for deceased users. Some allow memorialisation. Some allow verified family members or executors to request removal. Some provide limited access. Some do not provide access to private messages. Some require legal documents. Some accounts may be impossible to transfer in the way people expect. Policies change, so your plan should not rely on exact platform rules staying the same forever.

Instead, your plan should express your wishes and point your family to the right starting point.

For example: "My Facebook profile may be memorialised. Please do not delete it until family photos have been reviewed." Or: "My LinkedIn profile can remain visible for six months, then should be closed or updated according to the platform process." Or: "My Instagram account contains family photos. Please download the archive before requesting closure." Or: "My X account should be deleted. No announcement is needed."

These are simple instructions, but they remove a lot of guesswork.

Public announcements deserve special attention. Many families now face the question of whether to post about a death online. Sometimes it is helpful. Social media may be the easiest way to reach friends, former colleagues, distant relatives, customers or community members. Sometimes it feels too exposed. A death is intimate, and not everyone wants it turned into a public post.

You can make this easier by stating your preference.

You might say that an announcement is appropriate after close family has been notified. You might say no public announcement should be made from your account. You might nominate who can post. You might provide a short draft message. You might say that business accounts should post a brief operational update, while personal accounts should remain quiet.

This may feel unnecessary while you are alive, but it can be a relief later. People often worry about getting the tone wrong. They worry about who should speak. They worry about posting too soon, too late, too much or not at all.

A short instruction helps.

Something like: "If needed, a simple announcement may be posted after close family and friends have been told privately. Please keep it brief and do not share medical details." That sentence gives people permission and boundaries.

The same applies to private messages. Social platforms are not only public profiles. They are also communication archives. Direct messages may contain friendships, family conversations, business leads, customer questions, private jokes, old conflicts, intimate material and information about accounts or commitments. Your family may feel tempted to search messages for practical information, especially if they cannot find documents elsewhere.

This is where privacy becomes real.

Your plan can make a distinction between public content and private messages. You may be comfortable with family preserving photos but not reading DMs. You may want business messages reviewed by a business partner but personal messages left alone. You may want private messages deleted unread unless there is a legal or financial reason to review them.

These wishes may not always be perfectly enforceable, but writing them down still matters. It gives the people you trust a standard to follow.

Creators, authors and business owners need a more detailed approach. If your social presence supports income, reputation or customer relationships, it should be treated as part of the business or professional estate. A YouTube channel, Substack, Medium account, podcast feed, author profile, Instagram shop, LinkedIn following, online course platform or community account may have value. It may also require ongoing communication.

If you publish online, your family may need to know which accounts are simply personal and which are part of your work. They may need to preserve access, stop scheduled posts, respond to customers, pause advertising, transfer ownership, update bios, collect royalties or maintain the archive.

Scheduled content is easy to forget. Many creators and businesses use scheduling tools for social media posts, newsletters, podcasts, videos or marketing campaigns. If something happens to you, posts may continue going out automatically. In some cases, that may be harmless. In others, it may be painful or inappropriate.

Your plan should identify any scheduling tools and say who can pause them.

This is also important for paid advertising. Ads may keep running. Budgets may keep spending. Products may keep selling. Customers may keep buying. If there is no one monitoring the system, a business can drift into a strange half-alive state where money moves, customers receive no support and public accounts look active even though nobody is in control.

This is not rare anymore. Digital systems are built to keep running.

Estate plans need to know how to stop them.

Personal websites and domains also belong in this chapter because they are part of your public identity. A website may be a portfolio, business hub, author page, family project, blog, publication, shop or professional profile. It may be hosted through one company, registered through another, connected to email through a third and paid for by a credit card your family intends to cancel.

If the website matters, document the domain registrar, hosting provider, email provider, payment method, website platform and nominated person who should manage it. If it does not matter, say whether it should expire, be archived or be closed.

A domain name can be surprisingly important. It may control email addresses, business credibility, published work, search results, customer access or intellectual property. Losing a domain accidentally because an annual renewal failed can create problems long after the estate should have been settled.

If you have a public website, do not leave it as a mystery.

Online identity also includes profiles you may not think of as social media. Professional directories, Google Business Profile, review platforms, marketplace seller profiles, GitHub, portfolio sites, speaking profiles, industry memberships, alumni pages, online learning profiles and community accounts may all present you publicly. Some should remain. Some should be updated. Some should be closed. Some may contain work or records worth preserving.

The point is not to obsess over every trace of yourself online. That way lies madness and a very long spreadsheet. The point is to identify the profiles that someone else might reasonably need to manage.

A useful rule is this: if the account affects your reputation, income, customers, family memories, legal obligations or public identity, include it.

Everything else can be lower priority.

There is another risk: impersonation and account takeover. Dormant profiles can be targeted. A public account that remains online but unmanaged may be hacked, used for scams or exploited to contact friends and family. This is especially concerning after someone dies because friends may be more emotionally vulnerable to messages that appear to come from or relate to the deceased person.

A memorialised or properly closed account may reduce that risk. So can strong passwords, two-factor authentication and clear instructions. But the estate plan should also tell family which accounts are public enough to monitor or close.

Do not assume an account is safe because nobody has posted for years. Dormant accounts can still be useful to attackers if they have trust attached to them.

The emotional side is just as important. Social media platforms are designed to remind people. Birthdays. Memories. Anniversaries. Old posts. Suggested tags. Friend recommendations. These features can be comforting for some and distressing for others. Your family may have to decide whether a profile staying visible helps or hurts.

You cannot predict everyone's grief. But you can say what you prefer.

Some people want their profiles preserved as a record. Others would prefer their accounts deleted. Some want photos saved first. Some want professional work left online but personal accounts closed. Some want family to decide. All of these are valid. The problem is not the preference. The problem is never stating one.

A good instruction can be as simple as: "Preserve my writing and professional work. Download family photos where possible. Delete personal social accounts after family has had time to save anything meaningful." That is not legal poetry, but it is useful.

It gives direction without pretending to control every detail.

You should also separate ownership from access. Just because someone can log into an account does not mean they should take it over. Some platforms prohibit transfer. Some accounts are personal by design. Some business accounts can be transferred or assigned. Some creator accounts may have monetisation, contracts or intellectual property attached. The right path may involve platform policies, legal documents or business agreements.

This is why your plan should avoid telling people to simply log in and act as you. In some cases, that might be acceptable. In others, it may be inappropriate. Better wording is: "Follow the provider's process where required. Access instructions are available only to preserve records, secure the account or assist with closure."

This protects both your family and the estate.

There is one more category that deserves care: group chats and communities. WhatsApp groups, Discord servers, Slack workspaces, Facebook groups, online forums, gaming communities and private memberships may contain relationships that do not exist anywhere else. If you run a group, moderate a community or act as an administrator, someone may need to know how to transfer responsibility. If you are simply a member, the account may not matter.

The key question is whether other people depend on your role.

If they do, include it.

If you manage an online community, your plan should name another admin or explain who should be contacted. Communities can become fragile when the person holding access disappears. A paid group may need refunds, announcements or a new owner. A volunteer community may simply need another moderator. A business Slack may need admin transfer. A family WhatsApp group may need nothing at all.

Again, context matters.

By this point in the book, you may notice the same pattern repeating. We are not trying to make your family experts in every platform you use. We are giving them a map, a priority order and a set of boundaries.

For social media and public profiles, the map should answer:

  • Which accounts represent me publicly?
  • Which accounts contain material worth preserving?
  • Which accounts support income, business or professional identity?
  • Which accounts should be memorialised, deleted, archived or transferred?
  • Who is allowed to post, announce, respond or manage?
  • Which private areas should be left alone?

With those answers, your family has enough context to make better decisions.

The word "legacy" often gets used in a grand way, as if every person needs to design a public monument. Most people do not need that. They need something quieter and more practical. They need to decide what should stay visible, what should be saved and what should be allowed to end.

Not everything needs to remain online forever.

Not everything needs to disappear either.

The aim is to leave fewer awkward decisions for the people who care about you.

A public profile can be a useful archive, a professional record, a source of comfort or a loose end. Sometimes it is all four. The only way your family will know which one it is supposed to be is if you tell them.

Chapter 10

Crypto, Digital Assets and High-Risk Access

Some parts of a digital estate are inconvenient when they are poorly planned. Crypto and certain high-risk digital assets are different. They can be unforgiving.

A forgotten password to a streaming service is annoying. A lost seed phrase can mean an asset is gone permanently. A missed domain renewal can break a website or email system. An inaccessible royalty account can leave income unclaimed. A payment account no one knows about can keep receiving money while no one knows how to recover it. A crypto wallet can hold real value, yet be invisible to the ordinary estate process unless someone knows it exists and understands what kind of access is required.

This chapter is not about whether crypto is a good investment. It is not about price, speculation or ideology. It is about a narrower and more practical question: if you hold digital assets that require special access, would the right person be able to identify, protect and recover them without accidentally destroying them?

For many people, the answer is no, not because they are careless, but because high-risk digital assets do not behave like traditional assets. A bank account can usually be found through statements, tax records or institutional searches. A broker can be contacted. An insurer can confirm a policy. A superannuation or pension provider can follow a formal process. These systems can still be slow and frustrating, but they have procedures.

Self-custodied crypto is different. If you hold assets in your own wallet, there may be no company that can reset access, reverse a mistake or recognise your executor. The wallet may not know who you are. The blockchain may not know you died. The assets may sit behind a seed phrase, private key, hardware wallet, passphrase or multi-signature setup that either works or does not. If the recovery material is missing, wrong, damaged, misunderstood or thrown away, the asset may be unrecoverable.

This is the part people underestimate. Traditional estate planning often assumes that ownership can be proven later if the documents are right. With certain digital assets, technical access may be just as important as legal ownership. Your executor may have the legal right to deal with the estate and still be unable to move the asset. A court document does not unlock a wallet. A death certificate does not recreate a seed phrase. A will does not help if nobody knows the hardware device in the drawer is important.

This is why crypto and similar assets need a different standard of planning. You are not only documenting what exists. You are designing a recovery path that is secure while you are alive and usable after you are gone.

Those two goals are often in tension. If you make access too easy, you increase the risk of theft. If you make access too hard, you increase the risk of permanent loss. A seed phrase written on paper and left in a desk drawer might help your family later, but it could also expose you today. A seed phrase stored only in your memory protects you from theft in one sense, but it almost guarantees loss if something happens to you. A hardware wallet in a safe may be secure, but not if nobody knows the PIN, recovery phrase or passphrase. A sophisticated multi-signature setup may be excellent security for someone who understands it, but impossible for a grieving family member to navigate without instructions.

The aim is controlled recoverability, not maximum secrecy.

Before going further, it helps to separate the different types of digital assets people often group together. There are crypto exchange accounts, where assets are held through a company. There are self-custodied wallets, where access depends on private keys or seed phrases. There are hardware wallets, which are physical devices used to sign transactions. There may be NFTs or tokenised assets. There may be domain names, websites, online businesses, royalties, creator accounts, marketplace balances, payment processors and other digital income streams. Some are financial assets. Some are operational assets. Some are identity assets. Some are sentimental or creative assets with little immediate market value but real personal importance.

They should not all be handled the same way.

An exchange account is closer to a financial platform. If you hold assets on Coinbase, Kraken, Binance, Independent Reserve, BTC Markets or another provider, the company may have a deceased estate process. Your family may need to know the provider exists, which email address is used, whether two-factor authentication is enabled, and where records can be found. They should usually contact the provider through the formal process rather than trying to log in as you.

A self-custodied wallet is different. There may be no help desk. There may be no recovery process. The seed phrase or private key is the access. Anyone who has it may be able to move the asset. Anyone who loses it may lose access. That makes the planning problem more delicate.

This is where many people make one of two mistakes. The first is leaving no instructions at all because they believe secrecy is the safest approach. The second is writing everything down in one place because they want to make things easy. Both can fail.

No instructions can turn an asset into a rumour. A family may know you once "had some Bitcoin" but not know where, how much, what wallet, which exchange, whether it was sold, whether it was on a device, whether a seed phrase exists or whether the old metal plate in the safe matters. They may spend months searching through devices, emails and drawers, unsure whether they are chasing something real.

Too much information in one place creates the opposite risk. A document that says "Bitcoin wallet seed phrase" followed by the full recovery phrase is not just an estate note. It is the asset. If someone copies it, the money can move. If someone photographs it, the money can move. If someone finds it during a burglary, the money can move. If it is stored in cloud notes or an email draft, it may be exposed by a compromised account.

Crypto planning has to respect that the instruction and the key are not the same thing.

The instruction tells someone what exists and how the recovery process works. The key or seed phrase gives control. Those should usually be protected differently.

A useful crypto estate instruction might say: "I hold Bitcoin in self-custody using a hardware wallet. The device is stored in the home safe. Recovery material is stored separately according to the sealed instructions in the estate folder. Do not enter seed words into any website. Do not respond to anyone offering recovery help online. Contact [trusted person or professional] before moving funds."

Notice what that does. It identifies the asset type, warns against common mistakes, points to the recovery process and slows people down before they do something irreversible. It does not expose the seed phrase in the general inventory.

That warning about websites matters. One of the greatest risks after death is not only loss. It is theft by confusion. A family member who does not understand crypto may search online for "recover hardware wallet" or "restore Bitcoin wallet" and land on a scam. They may type seed words into a fake support site. They may reply to someone in a forum. They may install malicious software. They may move assets to the wrong address. They may trust a stranger because the stranger sounds technical.

Your plan should assume the person reading it may be intelligent, honest and completely unfamiliar with the mechanics.

Plain warnings are not overkill. They are kindness.

For example:

  • never type seed words into a website
  • never share a seed phrase by email, message or phone
  • never accept help from strangers online
  • never move funds without understanding the destination address
  • never throw away hardware wallets, metal backups or recovery cards
  • never reset a device unless the recovery phrase has been confirmed
  • contact the nominated person before attempting recovery

This is not paranoia. It is basic operational control.

If you use a hardware wallet, document the brand, approximate location, what assets it controls and where the recovery instructions are stored. Do not assume the device itself is enough. A hardware wallet can be lost, damaged or locked. The recovery phrase is often more important than the device. If there is an additional passphrase, sometimes called a twenty-fifth word, that must be handled with even more care. A seed phrase without the passphrase may not recover the wallet people expect.

If you use multi-signature custody, the plan needs to be clearer still. Multi-signature wallets can be excellent because they require multiple keys to move funds, reducing the risk that one stolen key compromises everything. But they also create more recovery complexity. Your family may need to know how many keys exist, where they are, which signing devices are involved, whether a coordinator file or wallet descriptor is needed, and who can help. If that sentence sounds too technical, that is the point. A multi-signature setup without clear instructions can become a puzzle no one can solve.

For sophisticated setups, you may need a professional, a trusted technical contact or a custody provider as part of the plan. The person should be chosen carefully. They should not have unilateral access unless that is intentional. Their role should be documented. Your executor should know when to contact them and what authority they do or do not have.

You may also decide that self-custody is not the right estate solution for every asset. Some people prefer regulated custody, exchange custody, collaborative custody or professional custody because their family would not be able to manage private keys safely. That is a personal decision involving trade-offs. Self-custody gives control, but control includes the burden of recovery. Custody reduces some technical risk but introduces provider, legal, jurisdictional and counterparty considerations.

This book cannot choose that for you. It can only make the trade-off visible.

The same thinking applies to NFTs and tokenised assets, if you hold them. Some may have market value. Some may be sentimental. Some may be connected to memberships, communities, games, art, intellectual property or access rights. Your plan should record where they are held, what wallet controls them, whether any are valuable or meaningful, and whether they should be preserved, sold, transferred or ignored.

Again, avoid making the general inventory the key.

Domain names deserve similar respect, even though they are not usually discussed with the same drama as crypto. A domain can be a business asset, a publishing asset, an email asset or a reputation asset. If it expires, someone else may buy it. If it controls email, losing the domain can break account recovery across multiple services. If it supports a business or author platform, losing it can damage years of work.

A domain is easy to overlook because it is often just a small annual charge. But small charges can support large systems.

Your plan should identify the registrar, renewal status, payment method, associated email, hosting provider, DNS provider if different, and what should happen to the domain. If the domain should be kept, say so. If it can expire, say so. If it should be transferred to a business partner, family member or buyer, say so.

Creator royalties and monetised accounts also belong here. Amazon KDP, Apple Books, Kobo, IngramSpark, YouTube, Substack, Medium Partner Program, Patreon, Spotify for Podcasters, affiliate platforms, course platforms, Gumroad, Shopify, Etsy, stock photo sites, music platforms and advertising networks can all contain income streams. Some may be modest. Some may matter. Some may continue paying after death.

These accounts are often missed because they do not look like bank accounts. They look like dashboards.

If you publish books, sell digital products, run a channel, write online, create templates, produce courses or operate a paid community, make a list of revenue platforms. Include the platform, login email, payment account, tax documents, approximate purpose, and who should handle it. If there are contracts, note where they are. If royalties should continue to a beneficiary or business entity, that belongs in your broader estate planning with professional advice.

The practical issue is that your family cannot collect, transfer or preserve income streams they cannot see.

Payment processors are similar. Stripe, PayPal, Square, Shopify Payments and marketplace systems may contain balances, customer obligations, refund exposure, tax records and ongoing payments. If these accounts are connected to a business, they should be part of the business continuity plan. If they are personal or creator-related, they still need to be identified.

Do not assume that because money eventually lands in a bank account, the source does not matter. The bank account shows deposits. It may not explain the business behind them, the customer obligations attached to them, or the platform that must be managed.

There is also a category of digital assets with little financial value but real operational value: software licences, cloud accounts, API keys, developer accounts, app stores, code repositories, databases and automation tools. For most people, these will not matter. For a developer, business owner, creator or operator, they may matter a lot. A GitHub account can hold code. An Apple Developer account can control apps. A Google Cloud or AWS account can run infrastructure. A Zapier or Make account can automate business processes. An email marketing platform can hold customer lists. A CRM can hold business relationships.

If shutting it down would affect customers, revenue, records or published work, include it.

High-risk access is not only about money. It is about irreversible consequences. Some mistakes can be fixed. Others cannot. You can reopen some subscriptions. You can request some records again. You can reset many passwords. But you may not be able to recover a lost seed phrase, reclaim an expired domain, restore a deleted cloud account, reconstruct a business database, retrieve funds sent to the wrong address, or recover an account after someone has closed it without preserving the data.

The plan should slow people down around irreversible actions.

A useful instruction might say: "Do not sell, transfer, reset, delete, wipe, close or allow to expire any account marked high-risk until the executor has reviewed the instructions and spoken with the nominated adviser or technical contact."

That may sound formal, but it is really just a pause button.

Many estate mistakes happen because people are trying to be efficient. They cancel the card. They close the account. They wipe the device. They throw away the hardware wallet. They ignore the domain renewal. They delete the email. They reset the phone. They sell the laptop. They do not know they are destroying access because nobody told them the object mattered.

A high-risk asset plan tells them where to pause before acting.

You should also decide how much detail belongs in the main inventory. For most people, the main inventory should identify the asset category and the location of secure instructions, not the sensitive secret itself. It might say:

  • crypto exchange accounts listed in financial inventory
  • self-custody wallet instructions stored in sealed envelope
  • hardware wallet located in safe
  • seed phrase backup stored separately
  • domain registrar listed in public profile plan
  • royalty platforms listed in income sources
  • technical contact: [name]

The secure instruction layer can then contain more detailed recovery steps.

This layered approach matters because your digital estate inventory may be read by people who do not need full access. An executor, family member, lawyer, accountant or technical helper may each need different information. Giving everyone everything increases risk. Giving no one enough creates failure. Layers allow you to share context without exposing control.

For crypto in particular, consider writing instructions at two levels.

The first level is plain English: what exists, what not to do, who to contact, where secure material is stored.

The second level is technical: wallet type, custody method, recovery process, passphrase notes, backup locations, exchange accounts, tax records and any steps required to recover safely.

The technical instructions should be tested carefully while you are alive. Not by exposing funds unnecessarily, but by confirming that the process makes sense. If you cannot explain your recovery setup to a trusted person or future professional, you may need to simplify it.

Complexity is not security if nobody can use it when it matters.

Tax records are another reason to document digital assets. Crypto transactions, NFT sales, creator royalties, marketplace income and digital business revenue can create tax obligations. Your family may need transaction histories, exchange statements, wallet records, purchase dates, sale dates, cost bases, income reports and accountant notes. If those records live only in your memory or inside platforms nobody can access, the estate may face unnecessary difficulty.

A plan should note where tax records are stored and which accountant or adviser understands them. If no one understands them yet, that is a gap worth closing.

There is also the issue of volatility. Some digital assets can move sharply in value. That does not mean your family should rush to sell or hold. It means they should know who has authority to decide and where advice should come from. Your estate documents, not a casual note in this book, should determine decision-making authority. The digital estate plan simply helps the authorised person find the asset and avoid technical mistakes.

Be careful with language. "Sell my Bitcoin immediately" or "Never sell this asset" may create problems depending on the estate, the law, tax, beneficiaries and market conditions. If you have strong wishes, discuss them with the appropriate adviser and include them in the right estate planning documents. The practical inventory can still say where the asset is and how it should be handled operationally.

That distinction between wishes, legal authority and technical access matters throughout this chapter.

You may wish for an asset to go to a particular person. Legal documents may need to support that. Technical access must then make recovery possible. If any one of those layers is missing, the plan may fail.

This is why high-risk digital assets should not be left until the end of life. They require calm setup. They require testing. They require secure storage. They require clear instructions. They may require professional advice. Trying to solve them during grief is asking too much of the people left behind.

The good news is that most people do not need an elaborate system. They need a clear list of high-risk assets, a warning around irreversible mistakes, secure storage for sensitive recovery material, and a nominated person or process for help.

For many households, that is enough. For more complex estates, it may not be. If you hold substantial crypto, operate online businesses, own valuable domains, earn creator royalties, manage software systems, hold digital intellectual property or run monetised platforms, you should treat this as a real part of estate planning, not as a hobby note or side document.

The value may not be obvious from the outside, which is exactly why it needs to be documented.

  • software licences
  • business databases

If an account holds money, controls income, supports a business, protects identity or may be hard to recover, include it.

  • on an exchange
  • in a software wallet
  • on a hardware wallet
  • through a custody provider
  • through a multi-signature setup
  • through another person, company or trust structure

The recovery process depends heavily on custody type.

  • where secure recovery instructions are stored

  • what should not be done
  • whether legal or tax advice is required

The general inventory should point to the secure process. It should not become the key.

  • Do not close royalty or payment accounts until balances, tax records and income streams are reviewed.

Write down who can assist if needed.

Include:

  • trusted technical contact
  • accountant
  • lawyer
  • financial adviser
  • business partner
  • custody provider
  • platform support process

Clarify what each person can help with. Technical skill does not automatically give authority over assets.

For each high-risk asset or platform, note where records can be found.

Examples:

  • transaction history
  • royalty reports
  • exchange statements
  • wallet records
  • invoices
  • customer records
  • tax summaries
  • accountant notes
  • contracts

This will help the estate deal with tax and reporting obligations.

For each item, choose the first action:

  • contact provider
  • preserve access
  • speak to adviser
  • download records
  • maintain renewal
  • transfer ownership
  • pause payments or ads
  • review before deciding
  • do not touch until instructions are read

The first action matters because many mistakes happen before anyone understands the asset.

8. Write the one-sentence instruction

Finish with one clear sentence:

"My high-risk digital assets are listed in [location]. Sensitive recovery material is stored separately in [secure location]. Do not move, delete, reset, transfer, sell, close or allow these assets to expire until the secure instructions have been reviewed and the authorised person has confirmed the next step."

That sentence is not dramatic.

It is a guardrail.

Chapter 11

Who Should Be Allowed to Act?

A digital estate plan can list every account, every device and every important file, but it still depends on people. Someone has to read the instructions. Someone has to decide what matters first. Someone has to contact providers, preserve files, keep phone services active, speak to banks, avoid scams, respect privacy, ask for help and know when not to touch something.

That person may not be the same person for every task.

This is where estate planning often becomes too neat on paper. A will names an executor. A power of attorney names an attorney. A business may have a director, partner or manager. A family may have one person everyone trusts. The legal documents matter, and you should take them seriously. But digital life cuts across legal, technical, emotional and practical responsibilities in a way that traditional documents do not always capture cleanly.

The person with legal authority may not be technically confident. The person who understands technology may not be the person you want reading private messages. The person who knows your business may not be a family member. The person who should recover photos may not be the person who should deal with investments. The person who can find a seed phrase should not automatically have permission to move assets.

A good digital estate plan separates roles.

This is not about making the plan complicated. It is about avoiding the lazy assumption that one person can or should do everything. When one person is given the whole digital mess without guidance, they often become either overwhelmed or overpowered. They may avoid acting because they are afraid of doing the wrong thing, or they may act too quickly because they do not understand the consequences.

The better approach is to decide who should be allowed to act in each area and what kind of action they are allowed to take.

There are several roles to think about.

The first is the legal decision-maker. Depending on your circumstances, this may be your executor, administrator, attorney, trustee, guardian, director, business partner or another legally recognised person. The exact names and powers differ from place to place, but the practical point is consistent: someone needs formal authority to deal with banks, insurers, government agencies, property, financial accounts, business matters and many providers.

Your digital estate plan should support that person, not replace them. It should help them find accounts, understand dependencies and avoid unnecessary damage. It should not pretend that a password gives legal authority where the law requires something else.

The second role is the trusted personal helper. This may be a spouse, adult child, sibling, close friend or another person who understands your personal life. They may be the right person to recover family photos, identify sentimental files, manage private belongings, notify friends, handle social profiles or protect personal material. They may be emotionally close enough to understand what matters, but that closeness can also make the work painful.

The third role is the technical helper. This person understands devices, cloud storage, password managers, two-factor authentication, backups, domains, websites or crypto recovery. They may be excellent at solving access problems, but technical skill is not the same as judgement or authority. A technical helper should usually assist someone with authority rather than operate independently.

The fourth role is the business or professional contact. If you run a business, publish work, manage clients, operate online systems or have professional obligations, someone may need to understand that world quickly. This might be a business partner, accountant, operations manager, lawyer, publisher, assistant, contractor or trusted colleague. They may need to keep systems running, contact customers, pause marketing, preserve records or help the family understand what is urgent.

The fifth role is the adviser. Lawyers, accountants, financial advisers, insurance brokers, IT providers and other professionals can help interpret rules, handle provider processes and reduce risk. They are not always needed for everything, but they matter when decisions have legal, tax, financial or technical consequences.

Most estates need some mixture of these roles. The mistake is leaving the mixture undefined.

Sofia is the person everyone looks to first, but she has never used the password manager, does not know what a domain registrar is, feels uncomfortable searching email and has no idea whether a crypto hardware wallet is valuable. Her husband's friend understands all of that, but has no legal authority and no clear permission to help. Without instructions, both people may hesitate. Sofia feels exposed and unsupported. The friend worries about overstepping. Important accounts may sit untouched while everyone waits for someone else to decide.

A simple role map can prevent that.

The role map says: this person has legal authority, this person can help with technical recovery, this person should recover family photos, this person understands the business, this person should not access private material, this adviser should be contacted before financial or tax decisions, and this provider should be approached through its formal process.

That sounds more formal than it needs to be. In practice, it can be a one-page note.

For example:

"My executor is responsible for formal estate administration. My brother can assist with devices, password manager access and cloud storage, but only under the executor's direction. My partner should decide which family photos and personal files are preserved. My accountant should be contacted before business accounts or royalty platforms are closed. Private journals should be deleted unread."

There is no magic in that wording. Its value is that it removes uncertainty.

You also need to think about what each person should not do. Estate instructions often focus on permission, but limits are just as important. A technical helper may be allowed to copy photos but not read messages. A business contact may be allowed to pause scheduled content but not make financial decisions. A family member may be allowed to post a death notice but not take over an account. An executor may have legal authority but still be asked to respect private folders unless access is necessary.

Good instructions do not only open doors. They place boundaries around the rooms.

This is especially important because grief changes behaviour. People who are usually careful may rush. People who are usually patient may panic. People who are usually private may search everything because they are frightened of missing something. People who are usually trusting may become suspicious. Family dynamics that were manageable while everyone was alive can become sharper after death.

A digital estate plan cannot fix family conflict, but it can reduce the number of decisions people have to invent under pressure.

Authority should also be staged. Not every person needs access immediately. Some people need to know the plan exists. Some need access only after death. Some need access during incapacity. Some need access only if the executor asks. Some should never receive passwords but may provide advice. Some should receive copies of certain files but not the entire archive.

This staged approach is safer than giving broad access in advance. While you are alive, you may want someone to know that a sealed instruction exists without being able to open it. You may want a lawyer to hold a document, a spouse to know where it is, and a technical helper to be named inside it. No single person needs every piece today.

After death or incapacity, the plan can come together.

This is also where you should revisit emergency access features. Password managers, cloud accounts and some platforms allow trusted contacts or legacy contacts. These tools can be useful, but they are not a full estate plan. A legacy contact may be able to access photos but not email. A password manager emergency contact may access credentials but not know which accounts require formal legal processes. An Apple legacy contact may help with certain data, but not every service connected to your Apple ID. A Facebook legacy contact may manage memorialisation, but not private messages.

Use these tools where they help, but document what they do and what they do not do.

You should also tell the person if they have been named. This seems obvious, but many people skip it because the conversation feels awkward. They quietly nominate someone as an emergency contact, executor, password manager access person or technical helper and never explain the role. Then, when the time comes, that person receives a notification, a request or a responsibility they did not expect.

It is not fair to leave someone with a responsibility they did not know they had. You do not need to reveal every detail. You can simply say, "I am putting together a digital estate plan. I would like you to be the person who helps with technical access if something happens to me. You would not be making legal decisions. You would be helping my executor understand devices, cloud storage and accounts. Are you comfortable with that?"

That question respects the person.

It also tests whether your plan is realistic. Someone may love you and still not want the responsibility. Someone may be technically capable but emotionally unable to help. Someone may be willing but not available. Someone may be the right person today but not in five years.

Roles need maintenance just like passwords and account lists do.

When Family Members Disagree

Digital estates can create conflicts that ordinary estate documents do not always anticipate. The fight is not always about money. Sometimes it is about memory, privacy, grief or control.

One adult child may want to keep a parent's Facebook profile online because friends still leave messages there. Another may want it deleted because seeing new comments each birthday feels unbearable. A spouse may want every photo, message and note preserved because deleting anything feels like another loss. An executor may want to close accounts quickly because bills, renewals and administrative tasks keep piling up. A business partner may want access to customer records while the family is still deciding what belongs to the business and what belongs to the person.

Nobody in that situation has to be unreasonable for the situation to become difficult.

Mark's family shows why this matters. His wife wants the family photo archive preserved. His adult daughter wants his private messages left alone. His son believes the crypto wallet should be dealt with immediately because the value may move. His executor wants to wait until formal authority is clear. Without instructions, each person can argue that they are protecting something important.

The conversation does not sound like a legal dispute at first. It sounds like grief.

"We can't delete his account," his wife says. "People are still writing to him."

"I don't want everyone reading his messages," his daughter replies. "He would have hated that."

His son is looking at a hardware wallet on the kitchen table. "What if this is worth something and we leave it too long?"

The executor is not trying to be difficult. She is trying to slow the room down. "I don't know who is allowed to touch what yet."

No one is wrong. That is the problem. Everyone is protecting a different value: memory, privacy, money and authority. A plan written earlier could have named the order of decisions before the family had to invent one under pressure.

A digital estate plan should not try to control every future emotion, but it can set decision rules before grief enters the room.

Useful rules include:

  • preserve before deleting
  • copy family memories before closing storage accounts
  • use official provider processes for financial accounts
  • do not read private messages unless needed for administration
  • keep business records separate from personal files
  • ask the nominated technical helper before touching high-risk assets
  • let the legal decision-maker decide if family members disagree

The most helpful instruction is often a default. "If there is disagreement, preserve the account or files until the executor has spoken with the relevant family members." Or: "Family photos should be copied for immediate family before any cloud account is closed." Or: "Private journals and personal messages should not be reviewed unless needed to locate financial, legal or family records."

These sentences are not sentimental. They are practical. They give people something to lean on when their own judgement is tired.

The Chen family used a different rule because a business was involved: customer, supplier, payment and tax records came first; personal material was not to be searched unless a specific business reason existed. That distinction protected the business without turning illness into an excuse for everyone to browse everything.

Conflict usually grows in empty space. If no one knows what the person wanted, everyone fills the silence with their own version of care. A plan cannot remove grief, but it can remove some of the guessing.

The legal side deserves careful handling. Your will, power of attorney, enduring guardian documents, trust documents, company documents and beneficiary nominations may determine who can act. Digital instructions should align with those documents. If your digital estate plan says one thing and your legal documents say another, confusion follows.

You do not need to turn every digital instruction into legal language, but your formal documents should give the right people authority where authority is required. In some places, digital assets, online accounts and access rights may need specific wording. Platform terms may restrict access. Privacy laws may matter. Business assets may belong to a company, not you personally. Beneficiary nominations may override assumptions.

A practical digital plan is strongest when it sits beside proper legal planning. Think of the digital plan as the operating manual: the legal documents give authority, while the digital plan shows where things are, what they do, who can help and what to avoid.

There is also a difference between access and ownership. This is worth repeating because it is one of the easiest mistakes to make. If someone has a password, they may be able to log in. That does not mean they own the account, have permission to act, or should impersonate the deceased person. Access is a technical ability. Authority is a legal or ethical right. Your plan should not blur the two.

For example, an executor may need to identify a bank account but should contact the bank through the estate process. A family member may need to download photos but should not read private messages. A business partner may need to preserve customer records but should not treat personal files as business property. A technical helper may need to unlock a device but should not browse beyond the agreed purpose.

Clarity protects the estate from mistakes, private information from unnecessary exposure and helpers from being accused of overstepping. It also protects the person who is grieving and trying to do the right thing.

One useful phrase is "under the direction of." A technical helper can assist under the direction of the executor. A business contact can provide information under the direction of the person legally responsible. A family member can help preserve photos under the direction of the executor or spouse. This keeps roles connected to authority rather than floating loosely.

You may also want to include a "decision order." If there is disagreement, who decides? If one person is unavailable, who is next? If a technical helper cannot assist, who else can be contacted? If an account contains both business and personal material, who should review it first? If something is unclear, should the default be preserve, pause, delete or seek advice?

Defaults prevent rushed decisions. For digital estates, a sensible starting point is often: preserve first, decide later. Do not delete, reset, cancel, transfer or discard until the account or device has been understood. This does not mean keeping everything forever. It means avoiding irreversible action during the fog.

Another useful default is: use official provider processes for financial, legal and regulated accounts. If a bank, insurer, broker, superannuation fund, pension provider or government agency has a deceased estate process, use it. Do not rely on passwords simply because they are available.

A third default is: protect privacy unless access is necessary. Do not search personal material just because it is technically accessible. Look for what is needed, preserve what has been requested, and leave the rest alone where possible.

These defaults can be written into your plan.

The most important thing is that the people involved know the plan exists. A brilliant document hidden too well is almost the same as no document. Someone trusted should know where to begin. They do not need every secret. They need the first step.

That first step might be: "The digital estate inventory is in the estate folder." Or: "The sealed access instructions are in the safe." Or: "My lawyer holds the digital estate letter." Or: "The password manager emergency access process is documented with my will." Or: "Call [person] before touching devices." The beginning should be easy, even if the rest of the plan is more secure.

There is a human kindness in this. You are not only choosing people to perform tasks. You are trying to spare them from feeling lost. After someone dies, people often want to help but do not know what help looks like. A good plan gives them useful roles without asking them to carry everything alone.

It says: you handle this. You ask this person for help. You do not need to open that. You can wait on this. Call the provider for that. Preserve this before cancelling anything. Do not make this decision alone.

That kind of direction is a gift.

It is also a reminder that digital estate planning is not really about technology. Technology is the environment. People are the system. The plan works only if the right people can understand it, trust it and act on it. Choose them carefully, tell them enough, give them boundaries and make the work easier than it would have been without you.

  • what their role is not
  • who has authority
  • who else may be involved

A person should not discover a serious responsibility for the first time during a crisis.

7. Write the one-sentence instruction

Finish with one clear sentence:

"My digital estate should be handled by [legal decision-maker], with help from [trusted helpers] for [specific areas]. Technical access does not automatically mean authority, and private material should be reviewed only when necessary for estate administration or according to these instructions."

That sentence gives the plan a chain of responsibility. It tells people who leads, who helps and where the boundaries are.

Chapter 12

The Digital Estate Inventory

At this point, the idea of a digital estate can start to feel large. Passwords. Email. Phones. Two-factor authentication. Photos. Files. Subscriptions. Financial accounts. Social profiles. Domains. Crypto. Business systems. People. Permissions. Privacy. It is easy to look at the list and feel as if the solution must be a huge document, maybe a spreadsheet with hundreds of rows, colour-coded tabs and enough detail to make it feel official.

That would be a mistake.

A useful digital estate inventory is not a database of your entire life. It is a map. Its job is to help the right person understand what exists, where to begin and what not to do too quickly. It should be detailed enough to reduce confusion, but not so detailed that it becomes impossible to maintain or dangerous to store.

This distinction matters because many people either do too little or try to do too much. Doing too little leaves your family guessing. Doing too much creates a fragile document that becomes outdated, exposes sensitive information or overwhelms the person who needs to use it. The right inventory sits between those extremes.

It should be clear, practical and boring in the best possible way.

Think of the inventory as the front desk of your digital estate. It does not contain every room key. It tells someone which rooms exist, which ones matter, who is allowed to enter, and where the proper keys are kept. That is enough to begin.

The inventory should answer five questions for each important item.

What is it?

Why does it matter?

Where can it be found?

How should access be handled?

What should happen next?

If an entry answers those questions, it is probably useful. If it does not, it may be clutter.

The first question, "what is it?", sounds obvious, but many digital accounts become confusing because the names are not clear. A charge on a bank statement may say "Google," but that could mean cloud storage, app subscriptions, advertising, business email, YouTube Premium or something else. A domain registrar may have a name your family has never heard of. A payment processor may be connected to business income. A cloud folder may contain family photos, tax documents or both.

Use plain names. "Google account used for Gmail and family photos" is better than "Google." "Domain registrar for rickyrecalcati.com" is better than "Namecheap." "Password manager storing most account credentials" is better than "1Password." "Cloud storage holding family archive" is better than "Dropbox."

The person reading the inventory may not know your systems, so give them context.

The second question is "why does it matter?" Not every account deserves the same attention. Some accounts matter because they hold money. Some because they hold memories. Some because they control access. Some because they keep a business running. Some because they should be closed. Some because they are private and should be handled carefully.

If you do not explain why something matters, the reader has to guess.

A good inventory entry might say: "iCloud Photos. Holds main family photo library. Preserve before closing Apple account." Or: "Mobile phone plan. Receives SMS codes for banking and email recovery. Do not cancel until two-factor authentication has been reviewed." Or: "Amazon KDP. Receives book royalties and tax documents. Contact through formal account process and speak to accountant before changes."

These explanations are short, but they make the decision clearer.

The third question is "where can it be found?" This may mean a website, app, folder, device, filing cabinet, safe, adviser, email address or provider. You are not always giving access here. You are giving location.

For an account, location might be the provider name and login email. For files, it might be the cloud folder or external drive. For documents, it might be the estate folder, lawyer's office or accountant's portal. For a device, it might be the home office drawer. For a hardware wallet, it might be the safe, while the recovery instructions are stored separately.

Location is often more valuable than people realise. Families lose enormous time not because the asset is impossible to access, but because nobody knows where to look.

The fourth question is "how should access be handled?" This is where safety matters. The answer may be "through the official provider process," "credentials stored in password manager," "emergency access instructions in sealed envelope," "ask technical helper," "do not attempt access without adviser," or "no access needed, contact provider only."

This field prevents one of the most common mistakes: assuming that finding a password means using it. Sometimes access is appropriate. Sometimes it is not. Sometimes the right action is to preserve evidence, then contact the provider formally. Sometimes the account contains private material and should only be searched for specific records. Sometimes a technical helper should assist but not act independently.

Access instructions should be clear without exposing unnecessary secrets.

The fifth question is "what should happen next?" This turns the inventory from a list into a plan. The next action may be preserve, close, transfer, download, contact provider, review, maintain subscription, memorialise, delete unread, seek advice or leave alone.

Next actions are useful because the person handling the estate may be tired and overwhelmed. They do not want to interpret your whole digital life from first principles. They need the first sensible step.

A good next action might be: "Preserve until family photos are downloaded." Or: "Contact insurer to confirm policy." Or: "Close after tax records are retrieved." Or: "Do not cancel until business email has been transferred." Or: "Review with accountant before making changes."

These are practical, not perfect. Practical is what you want.

The inventory itself can be simple. A spreadsheet works. A table in a document works. A printed worksheet works. A secure note can work if it is stored properly and someone knows how to find it. A password manager secure note can work for some people, although you still need a way for someone to know it exists. The format matters less than the usefulness.

  • Item
  • Why it matters

  • Notes

The most important design principle is separation. Your inventory should not contain everything sensitive. It should not become a master password list, seed phrase vault, recovery code dump or private diary. If someone finds the inventory, they should understand your digital estate, not automatically control it.

This is where the layered approach matters.

Layer one is the map: the inventory.

Layer two is the secure access material: password manager, sealed envelope, lawyer-held instructions, safe, secure note or other protected system.

Layer three is the legal authority: will, power of attorney, trust documents, company documents, beneficiary nominations and provider processes.

The inventory points to the other layers. It should not replace them.

For example, the inventory might say: "Password manager: 1Password. Holds most credentials. Emergency access instructions stored in sealed envelope in home safe." It should not list the master password in the same row. For crypto, it might say: "Hardware wallet in safe. Recovery material stored separately. Do not type seed words into any website. Contact nominated technical adviser before attempting recovery." It should not print the seed phrase in the inventory.

This keeps the document useful without making it reckless.

The inventory should also avoid false precision. Do not spend hours recording balances that will be wrong next month. Do not list every subscription renewal date unless it genuinely matters. Do not document every minor online account. Do not turn the inventory into an administrative burden you will abandon.

Focus on accounts and assets that meet at least one of these tests:

  • Would my family need to find it?
  • Would losing access cause financial, practical or emotional harm?
  • Does it control access to other accounts?
  • Does it contain money, records, photos, identity, business value or private material?
  • Should it be closed, preserved, transferred, deleted or handled carefully?

If the answer is no to all of those, it probably does not belong in the main inventory.

There is a freedom in leaving things out.

Not every login deserves a line. Not every app matters. Not every website account needs attention. The inventory is not a confession booth for your internet history. It is an operating guide for the people who may need to deal with your affairs.

This also helps with privacy. A shorter, purpose-built inventory is less likely to expose things unnecessarily. It tells people where the important items are without inviting them into every corner of your life.

For private material, be direct. If there are folders, journals, messages or files that should not be reviewed unless legally necessary, include that instruction. If certain material should be deleted unread, say so. If business client records are confidential, say who should handle them. If medical documents should go only to a specific person, say that.

People cannot respect boundaries they cannot see.

The inventory should also identify your "do not cancel yet" items. These are services that may look optional but support something important. A phone plan that receives two-factor codes. Cloud storage that holds photos. A domain that controls email. A password manager subscription. Website hosting. Accounting software. Business email. A backup service. A paid email account.

These should be easy to spot. You might mark them as "critical" or "preserve temporarily." The label matters less than the warning. The person handling your affairs should be able to see, quickly, which services should not be shut down during the first wave of cleanup.

Another useful label is "high-risk." Use it for anything where mistakes could be costly or irreversible: crypto wallets, seed phrases, hardware wallets, valuable domains, business systems, payment processors, royalty platforms, cloud accounts with irreplaceable files, developer accounts or anything that requires technical help.

The high-risk label should slow people down.

It should say, in effect, read the instructions before touching this.

A good inventory also includes advisers and helpers. If you have an accountant, lawyer, financial adviser, insurance broker, IT provider, business partner, technical helper or trusted contact, list them. Include what they know, not only their name. "Accountant: handles business tax and knows Xero." "Lawyer: holds will and estate documents." "Technical helper: understands website, domains and email hosting." "Business contact: can help identify urgent customer obligations."

This prevents your family from having to reconstruct your support network.

If you run a business, consider creating a separate business section or separate business inventory. Personal and business digital assets often overlap, especially for small business owners, creators and sole traders. But they should not be hopelessly mixed. A family member looking for photos should not have to navigate client records. A business partner looking for domain access should not be reading personal notes.

Separate the worlds where possible.

For a business, the inventory should identify systems that affect continuity: email, website, domain, accounting, payroll, bank accounts, payment processors, customer records, supplier records, passwords, social accounts, advertising accounts, cloud storage, software subscriptions, staff contacts, contractor contacts, insurance and adviser details.

The first question for business systems is not "What is everything?" It is "What must not fail in the first week?"

That may be payroll, customer communication, bookings, supplier payments, website checkout, email, rent, insurance, or access to records. Identify those first.

The inventory can become more detailed later.

Version control matters too. If you create a digital estate inventory, date it. Put a "last reviewed" date at the top. This tells the reader how much confidence to place in it. A plan reviewed three months ago is different from one last touched eight years ago. Even an old plan may be useful, but the date gives context.

You might also include a short note: "If this document is more than two years old, treat it as a starting point rather than a complete record." That kind of note is honest and useful.

The inventory should be reviewed at least once a year, and whenever something major changes: new phone, new password manager, new bank, new business, new cloud storage, new domain, new crypto setup, new adviser, new relationship, new executor, major move, marriage, divorce, birth, death, business sale or serious health event.

Do not let that list intimidate you. Most reviews are quick once the first version exists. The first version is the hard one because you are making the invisible visible. After that, maintenance is much easier.

A good review asks:

  • Are the important accounts still listed?
  • Have any new gateway accounts been added?
  • Has the password manager changed?
  • Has the phone number or recovery email changed?
  • Are critical subscriptions still marked?
  • Are advisers and helpers still correct?
  • Are storage locations still accurate?
  • Are private instructions still what I want?
  • Are high-risk assets still documented safely?

This is fifteen minutes if the inventory is kept lean.

It is impossible if the inventory has become a monster.

When you build your first version, resist the urge to start with formatting. Do not spend the first hour choosing colours, tabs, fonts or template styles. Write the substance first. The inventory can be ugly and useful. Later, you can make it cleaner.

The first draft should answer: what would someone need to know if something happened to me this week?

That question cuts through the noise.

They would need to know where your email is, where your phone is, which bank you use, which subscriptions should not be cancelled, where the photos are, who your accountant is, whether there are business systems, whether there are high-risk digital assets, where the password manager instructions are and who can help.

Do not wait until you can finish the whole thing. A half-finished inventory is still better than no inventory if it identifies the most important accounts. You can build it in layers.

First pass: gateway accounts and devices.

Second pass: financial accounts and subscriptions.

Third pass: photos, files and documents.

Fourth pass: social profiles, domains and business systems.

Fifth pass: high-risk assets, advisers and privacy instructions.

This is how real planning happens. Not in one heroic afternoon, but in several honest passes.

The final inventory should feel like something a tired person could use. That is the standard. Not whether it impresses you. Not whether it looks official. Not whether it captures every account. Could someone grieving, confused and under pressure open it and know what to do next?

If yes, it works.

Chapter 13

Where to Store the Plan

A digital estate plan has to solve a strange problem: it must be easy enough for the right person to find and difficult enough for the wrong person to misuse.

Most documents only have to do one of those things. A shopping list can be easy to find. A passport can be hard to access. A digital estate plan has to be both visible and protected, which is why storage matters more than people expect.

If you hide the plan too well, it may never be found. If you leave it too exposed, it may create the very risk it was meant to reduce. A spreadsheet on your desktop called "All My Accounts" is convenient until someone steals your laptop or gains access to your cloud storage. A sealed envelope locked in a safe is secure until nobody knows the safe exists, nobody knows the code, or the person who knows the code dies first. A password manager secure note is elegant until your family cannot access the password manager. A document held by a lawyer is sensible until the lawyer does not know there is a separate digital instruction that needs attention quickly.

Storage is not just a location. It is a recovery design.

The first rule is that your plan should have layers. The main inventory, the sensitive access instructions and the formal legal documents do not all need to live in the same place. In many cases, they should not. The inventory is the map. It can be readable by the person responsible for understanding the estate. The access instructions are more sensitive. They may include password manager recovery steps, device passcodes, hardware wallet notes, backup code locations or other material that should not be casually visible. Legal documents provide authority and should be handled through proper estate planning channels.

When these layers are separated, the plan becomes safer. Someone can find the map without automatically gaining control of everything. The map can tell them that secure instructions exist and where to begin. The secure instructions can remain protected until the right condition is met. The legal documents can confirm who is allowed to act.

This is the balance: enough visibility to start, enough protection to prevent misuse.

There are several places a digital estate plan can live, and none of them is perfect. The right answer depends on your life, your family, your risk level and the complexity of your assets. The goal is not to find a flawless storage method. The goal is to choose a method that someone can actually use.

A printed estate folder is often the simplest starting point. It might sit in a home safe, filing cabinet or other known location. It can include the inventory, adviser details, printed copies of key documents, and instructions explaining where sensitive access material is stored. The advantage is that paper is easy to understand. A grieving family member does not need to know which app to open. They can hold the folder, read the instructions and start.

The disadvantage is that paper can be stolen, damaged, outdated or found by the wrong person. It can also become risky if it contains too much sensitive information. A printed inventory is usually fine. A printed list of all passwords, seed phrases and recovery codes is a different matter entirely.

If you use paper, be selective. Put the map in the folder. Keep the keys somewhere more secure.

A home safe can be useful, especially for sealed instructions, backup drives, hardware wallets, important documents and recovery envelopes. But a safe only works if the right person can access it when needed. If no one knows the safe exists, if the combination is unknown, or if the safe is treated as a mysterious object nobody wants to touch, it becomes a locked problem rather than a solution.

A safe also needs basic discipline. Do not put every secret loosely inside it without labels. If a future executor opens the safe and finds a hardware wallet, USB drives, envelopes, old phones, paper notes and legal documents with no explanation, they still have a puzzle. Label items in a way that helps without exposing too much. "Password manager emergency instructions." "External drive: family photos backup." "Hardware wallet: do not reset. Read high-risk asset instructions first." These labels can prevent mistakes without printing the secret on the outside.

A lawyer or solicitor can hold formal estate documents and, in some cases, a sealed letter of wishes or digital estate instruction. This has advantages. The documents are not sitting casually at home. There is a professional process. Your executor may already contact the lawyer after death. The lawyer can help ensure that instructions align with your will and other documents.

The limitation is timing and detail. Your family may need to preserve devices, phone plans, subscriptions and cloud accounts quickly, before formal estate administration is fully underway. If all digital instructions are locked inside a legal file that nobody opens for weeks, important access paths may be lost. Also, not every lawyer will want to hold highly sensitive technical material such as seed phrases or password lists, and in many cases that may not be wise anyway.

A lawyer-held document is useful, but it should not be the only practical starting point unless your family knows to contact the lawyer immediately.

A password manager can be an excellent place to store secure notes, account records and emergency access instructions. It is built for sensitive information. It can hold credentials, notes, recovery codes and structured records. Some password managers allow emergency access, shared vaults or designated recovery contacts. If you already use one well, it may become the backbone of your digital estate plan.

But password managers have the same basic problem as every secure system: someone has to get in. If your entire plan is inside the password manager and nobody can access it, the plan fails. If emergency access is set up but nobody knows how to request it, the plan is weaker than it looks. If the master password is stored nowhere except your memory, your family may be locked out of the very tool designed to help them.

A password manager is often a good vault, but it still needs an external signpost.

That signpost might be a note in the estate folder saying which password manager you use, who is nominated for emergency access, where sealed recovery instructions are stored, and what not to do. It should not need to reveal the master password in the general folder. It only needs to explain the path.

Cloud storage is convenient but should be used carefully. A folder in iCloud Drive, Google Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive can hold the inventory, document copies and supporting files. It can be updated easily. It can be shared with a partner or trusted person. It can be backed up. For many families, this is practical.

The risk is that cloud storage depends on account access, subscriptions and security. If the account is inaccessible, the plan may be inaccessible. If the folder is shared too widely, sensitive information may be exposed. If the cloud account is compromised, the plan may be stolen. If the subscription lapses, data may be at risk.

Cloud storage works best for the map and non-sensitive documents, not for naked secrets. If you store sensitive material there, it should be encrypted or protected to a standard appropriate to the risk. Most people will be better served by keeping the general inventory in cloud storage and storing access secrets elsewhere.

Encrypted files can be useful, but they introduce another password problem. If you create an encrypted PDF, encrypted archive or encrypted drive, someone needs the password. If that password is lost, the file may be useless. If the password is written beside the file, encryption does little. Encryption can be a good tool for technically confident people, but it should not be used just to make the plan feel sophisticated.

A secure system that nobody can operate is not secure in any practical sense. It is just locked.

Some people choose a hybrid approach, and for many households this is the most realistic answer. The estate folder contains the plain-language inventory, adviser details and instructions. The password manager holds credentials and secure notes. A sealed envelope in the safe contains emergency recovery information. The lawyer holds formal documents. Cloud storage contains copies of non-sensitive records and family files. A trusted person knows where the plan begins.

This sounds more complicated than it is. Each layer has a job.

The folder explains.

The password manager protects.

The safe preserves.

The lawyer formalises.

The cloud stores.

The trusted person starts.

That structure is usually stronger than trying to make one location do everything.

The next question is who should know where the plan is. At least one trusted person should know the plan exists and where to begin. In many cases, two people should know, especially if one may be unavailable, grieving heavily or not suited to the task. The person does not need every password or access detail today. They need to know the starting point.

You might say: "If something happens to me, the digital estate inventory is in the estate folder in the safe. The folder explains who to contact and where secure access instructions are stored." That is enough. You have not exposed the secrets. You have made the path discoverable.

This conversation can feel uncomfortable, but it is much kinder than leaving someone to search your house, phone and inbox without guidance.

You should also think about failure points. What happens if your house burns down? What happens if the safe cannot be opened? What happens if your spouse dies with you? What happens if your lawyer retires? What happens if your password manager account is inaccessible? What happens if your cloud storage account is closed? What happens if the only person who knows the plan exists forgets?

You do not need to build an elaborate disaster recovery system, but you should avoid single points of failure for the most important information. A physical inventory and a digital backup. A lawyer-held document and a home copy. A primary trusted person and a backup trusted person. A hardware wallet and separate recovery material. A cloud archive and an external drive. These combinations reduce fragility.

Do not overdo it. Too many copies create security problems. Too few create recovery problems. The right number is enough that the plan can survive ordinary failure without spreading sensitive material everywhere.

A useful test is this: if something happened to me tomorrow, would the right person know where to begin within 24 hours?

If the answer is yes, your storage system is probably workable.

If the answer is no, fix that before worrying about elegance.

Another test: if the wrong person found the first document, could they immediately take over my digital life?

If the answer is yes, the document contains too much.

The first document should guide. It should not unlock everything.

You should also make sure the plan is labelled in a way people recognise. A folder called "Digital Estate Plan" is better than "Admin." A sealed envelope labelled "Emergency Digital Access Instructions" is better than an unmarked envelope. A document titled "Read This First" inside the estate folder is better than a cryptic filename.

Clarity matters. This is not the place for cleverness.

Inside the folder or first document, include a simple order of operations. People under stress do not want to infer the sequence. Tell them what to do first.

For example:

  1. Do not cancel phone plans, cloud storage, password manager, domain names or business subscriptions until reviewed.
  2. Contact the executor and lawyer.
  3. Locate the digital estate inventory.
  4. Preserve phones, laptops, external drives and hardware keys.
  5. Contact nominated helpers.
  6. Use official provider processes for financial accounts.
  7. Review photos and files before closing cloud accounts.
  8. Follow high-risk asset instructions before touching crypto, domains or payment platforms.

That sequence may not fit every estate, but something like it will prevent common mistakes. It turns storage into action.

The storage plan should also include update instructions. If your inventory is digital, where is the current version? If there are printed copies, how do people know whether they are current? If you update the password manager but not the printed note, which one wins? If the lawyer holds an older copy, should your family treat it as legal authority, a backup or a historical reference?

Version confusion can cause real problems. Date every copy. Mark old copies as old. If you print a new version, destroy or clearly supersede the previous one. If the digital version is the master, say so. If the paper version is the master, say so.

A simple line at the top helps: "Master version: printed copy in estate folder. Last reviewed: [date]." Or: "Master version: secure note in password manager. Printed estate folder contains location instructions only. Last reviewed: [date]."

This is not bureaucratic fussing. It prevents people from following outdated instructions.

Finally, remember that storage is not a one-time decision. Your life changes. Your family changes. Your accounts change. Your risk changes. The storage method that works today may not work in five years. A rented apartment, a shared house, a new marriage, a divorce, children growing up, a business sale, a move overseas, a new adviser or a serious illness can all change what is sensible.

Review the storage plan when the estate inventory is reviewed. Ask whether the right people still know where to begin. Ask whether the sensitive material is still protected. Ask whether there are too many copies. Ask whether there is a single point of failure. Ask whether a tired person could still follow the plan.

The goal is not perfection. It is recoverability with restraint.

Your digital estate plan should not be a treasure hunt. It should not be a security leak. It should not depend entirely on memory. It should not require your family to guess which folder matters, which safe holds the envelope, which app contains the passwords or which adviser has the latest copy.

It should sit quietly in the background, findable when needed and safe until then.

That is the standard.

Not hidden.

Not exposed.

Ready.

  • What if the phone is lost?

Fix the most obvious weak points without creating unnecessary copies.

5. Label the plan clearly

Use plain labels:

  • Digital Estate Plan
  • Read This First
  • Emergency Digital Access Instructions
  • Password Manager Recovery Instructions
  • High-Risk Digital Asset Instructions
  • Family Photo Archive
  • Business Continuity Notes

Do not rely on vague filenames or unmarked envelopes.

6. Set the order of operations

Write the first actions someone should take.

Examples:

  • preserve devices
  • keep phone plan active temporarily
  • do not cancel critical subscriptions
  • contact executor
  • contact lawyer
  • locate inventory
  • contact technical helper
  • use official provider processes
  • review cloud photos before closing accounts

This gives people a calm beginning.

7. Date every version

Add:

  • created date
  • last reviewed date
  • master version location
  • backup copy location, if any

If you replace the plan, remove or mark old copies clearly.

8. Write the one-sentence instruction

Finish with one clear sentence:

"The starting point for my digital estate plan is [location]. The inventory, legal documents and sensitive access instructions are stored separately, and the people named in the plan know where to begin without having full access before it is needed."

That sentence proves the storage plan is doing its job. It gives the right people a beginning without turning the plan into an open door.

Chapter 14

How to Keep It Updated

The hardest part of a digital estate plan is not creating it. The hardest part is keeping it alive without turning it into another administrative burden you quietly avoid.

A plan that is perfect today can become unreliable surprisingly quickly. You change phones. You switch banks. You add two-factor authentication. You move photos to a new cloud service. You create a new email address. You cancel a subscription. You start a business. You close a business. You buy a domain. You publish something. You change accountants. You update your will. You get married, divorced, have children, lose someone, move house, change jobs, change passwords, change devices, change your mind.

Digital life moves.

A digital estate plan has to move with it.

This does not mean you should update the plan every time you download an app or change a password. That would be impossible and unnecessary. The goal is not constant maintenance. The goal is to notice the changes that would make the plan misleading, unsafe or incomplete if something happened to you.

There is a difference between noise and structure.

Noise is the daily churn: new apps, small subscriptions, password changes, file downloads, temporary accounts, routine transactions. Structure is the part of your digital life that other things depend on: primary email, phone number, password manager, financial providers, cloud storage, photo archive, business systems, domains, advisers, trusted helpers, high-risk assets and storage locations.

You do not maintain the noise. You maintain the structure.

That principle keeps the plan manageable.

If you change the password to an account stored in your password manager, you do not need to update the estate inventory. The password manager handles that. If you open a one-off shopping account, it probably does not belong in the inventory. If you start using a new bank, change your phone number, move your photos from iCloud to Google Photos, buy a hardware wallet, start receiving royalties, nominate a new executor or move the sealed recovery envelope, that matters.

The question is simple: would this change affect someone's ability to find, access, preserve or properly handle something important?

If yes, update the plan.

If no, let it go.

Many people avoid estate planning because they imagine the maintenance will be endless. It does not have to be. A lean plan is easier to maintain than an overbuilt one. This is another reason the inventory should not include every login, balance or minor subscription. The more unnecessary detail you add, the more stale detail you create.

A useful plan should survive ordinary life.

One way to keep it current is to set a review rhythm. Once a year is enough for most people. Choose a date you will remember: the start of the year, your birthday, tax time, the end of financial year, the same month you review insurance, or any other point that already has administrative weight in your life. Do not make it dramatic. Make it routine.

The annual review should not be a full rebuild. It should be a check.

Open the inventory. Read the first page. Ask whether the main accounts are still correct. Check the phone number, email, password manager, cloud storage, financial providers, subscriptions marked critical, advisers, helpers, storage location and high-risk assets. Confirm the "Read This First" instructions still make sense. Update the last reviewed date.

That may be all you need.

A good annual review can take less than an hour once the first plan exists.

If it takes all weekend, the plan is probably too complicated.

There should also be event-based updates. These are moments when life changes enough that waiting for the annual review is unwise. A new phone can affect two-factor authentication. A new email address can affect account recovery. A new password manager changes the access system. A new business changes financial and operational records. A new relationship or separation changes who should know what. A house move changes storage locations. A new child changes priorities. A serious illness changes urgency.

Treat these as triggers.

You do not need to update everything. Just update the part affected by the change.

If you change phones, review device access, authenticator apps, trusted devices, backup codes and phone plan instructions. If you change email, review gateway accounts and recovery addresses. If you move house, review the safe, folder, backup drives and who knows where the plan begins. If you change advisers, update contact details and what each adviser knows. If you start a business, add the systems that must not fail in the first week.

This is how maintenance stays sane: small updates when structure changes, not huge rewrites when guilt builds up.

It also helps to keep a short "change log" at the top or bottom of the inventory. Nothing elaborate. Just a few lines:

  • 12 March 2027: changed phone, updated two-factor notes
  • 4 July 2027: moved photo archive to Google Photos
  • 18 October 2027: changed accountant details
  • 2 January 2028: annual review completed, no major changes

A change log tells the future reader that the document has been maintained. It also helps you remember what changed and when.

Version control matters because old instructions can be worse than no instructions. If your plan says the sealed envelope is in the old safe but you moved it two years ago, your family may search the wrong place. If it says your photos are in iCloud but you moved everything to Google Photos, they may waste precious time. If it names a helper you no longer trust, it may create conflict. If it lists an old phone number for two-factor authentication, account recovery may fail.

Outdated plans create false confidence, which is why every version should be dated. If you print an updated inventory, mark it clearly as the current version and remove the old one. If old copies must remain somewhere, mark them as superseded. If the digital version is the master, say so. If the paper version is the master, say so.

Do not leave your family with three conflicting versions and no way to know which one matters.

There is a practical habit that helps: make updates where the plan already lives. If your master inventory is a spreadsheet, update that spreadsheet. If your plan is a printed folder, update the document and print the new copy. If your password manager holds secure notes, update the secure note. Avoid creating "temporary" notes in random places. Temporary notes become permanent confusion.

Another habit is to keep the plan boring. Boring documents get maintained. Overdesigned documents become precious and annoying. If every update requires reformatting a beautiful spreadsheet, you will avoid it. If the plan is plain and functional, you will actually use it.

The plan is not a presentation. It is equipment, and equipment should work.

There is also a psychological side to maintenance. A digital estate plan forces you to confront future absence, which is not something most people enjoy doing once, let alone every year. It is natural to avoid it. The way through that is to make the review ordinary. Put the kettle on. Open the document. Check the list. Update the date. Close it.

Do not turn it into a meditation on mortality every time.

Think of it as household maintenance. You check smoke alarms. You renew insurance. You update software. You review passwords. You service the car. You back up files. You update the estate plan. Not because disaster is expected, but because responsible systems are maintained before they are needed.

A plan can also be too current in the wrong way. Be careful about constantly adding detail because it feels productive. More detail is not always more useful. If every minor subscription, app and account gets added, the important items become harder to see. If every account has long notes, the reader may miss the urgent warnings. If every file path is recorded, the plan becomes stale quickly.

Use the annual review to remove as well as add.

Delete old accounts that no longer matter. Remove subscriptions that were cancelled. Update helper names. Simplify notes. Archive irrelevant sections. Clarify labels. Move items from critical to closed. Make the document easier for someone else to use.

Maintenance is not only about accuracy. It is about clarity.

This is especially important after big life events. Marriage, separation, divorce, death of a nominated helper, birth of a child, business changes, relocation, serious illness or conflict can all make old instructions inappropriate. Someone who was once the obvious trusted person may no longer be right. A former partner may still know too much. An old business partner may still be listed. A deceased adviser may remain in the plan. A parent who once helped may now need help themselves.

Estate plans are emotional documents disguised as administrative ones. The names inside them matter.

Review the people as carefully as the accounts.

Ask:

  • Do I still trust this person with this role?
  • Are they still willing?
  • Are they still capable?
  • Do they know they are named?
  • Is there a backup?
  • Would this instruction cause conflict?
  • Does it match my legal documents?

If the answer has changed, update the plan.

Security also needs review. A storage method that was safe once may become weaker over time. A safe combination may be known by too many people. A cloud folder may be shared too widely. A printed copy may sit in an exposed drawer. A sealed envelope may have been moved. A password manager emergency contact may no longer be appropriate. A device backup may not be running. A recovery email may be old. A phone number may have changed.

These are small gaps until they matter.

The annual review should include a security check:

  • Is the inventory protected but findable?
  • Are sensitive secrets still separate?
  • Is the password manager secure?
  • Is two-factor authentication active for important accounts?
  • Are backup codes stored safely?
  • Does the right person know where to begin?
  • Are there old copies that should be destroyed?
  • Are there accounts that need stronger protection?

Again, do not aim for perfection. Aim for fewer obvious weak points.

You should also test the plan lightly. This does not mean handing over passwords or performing a dramatic fire drill. It means asking whether the instructions make sense. Could someone locate the estate folder? Could they identify the password manager? Could they tell which phone plan should stay active? Could they find the photo archive? Could they contact the accountant? Could they see which subscriptions are critical?

If you have a trusted partner or helper, you might ask them to read the "Read This First" page and tell you what they would do next. Their confusion is useful. It shows where the plan is unclear while you are still here to fix it.

Do not be defensive when they miss something. If they miss it, the plan did not explain it well enough, which is the point of testing.

A good plan should not depend on your presence to interpret it.

There is one more maintenance problem: procrastination after partial progress. People create a first draft, feel better, then never return. The first draft may still help, but it may also become dangerously outdated if treated as finished forever.

The way to avoid this is to make the next review date part of the plan. Do not leave it vague. Write it at the top: "Next review: January 2028." Put a calendar reminder in your phone. Tie it to something you already do. If you use a task manager, create a recurring task. If you review finances quarterly, add a digital estate check once a year. If you do an annual personal review, include it there.

The plan should remind you to maintain it.

You might also create a small update checklist rather than rereading everything every time. Something like:

  • email still correct
  • phone still correct
  • password manager still correct
  • photo archive still correct
  • financial providers still listed
  • critical subscriptions still marked
  • advisers still correct
  • helpers still correct
  • storage location still correct
  • high-risk assets still correct
  • legal documents still aligned
  • last reviewed date updated

This turns maintenance from a vague responsibility into a repeatable process.

Businesses need a shorter review cycle. If your business depends on digital systems, annual may not be enough. Staff, tools, payment processors, domains, passwords, software, client records, tax obligations and bank accounts can change often. A quarterly review of critical business systems may be more appropriate, even if the personal estate plan is reviewed annually.

For business owners, the question is not only "What happens if I die?" It is "What happens if I cannot work for thirty days?" A digital plan that supports temporary incapacity may be more valuable than one designed only for death. If a manager, spouse or business partner can keep payroll, customer communication, supplier payments and key systems moving during a crisis, the business is more resilient.

A stale business plan can fail quickly, which is another reason maintenance matters.

The same applies to creators and investors with active platforms. If you publish regularly, earn royalties, run paid communities, manage domains, hold crypto or operate multiple financial accounts, the plan may need more frequent attention. Not because everything changes every month, but because the cost of missing a structural change may be higher.

You can keep this simple by creating a "critical systems" list and reviewing it more often than the full inventory. The full inventory might be annual. Critical systems might be quarterly. Password manager, phone, email, domains, payment processors, business email, cloud storage, financial accounts, crypto custody, and key advisers.

The exact rhythm should fit the complexity of your life.

The deeper point is that a digital estate plan is not a document you finish. It is a system you maintain lightly.

Lightly is the important word.

If the system becomes heavy, you will avoid it. If it is too thin, it will fail. The art is to make it useful enough to matter and simple enough to survive.

A good plan should age well. It should not depend on perfect memory, constant updating or heroic discipline. It should be structured around stable categories, clear instructions and a review rhythm that catches important changes.

That is how you keep it alive: not by obsessing over every account, but by reviewing the few things everything else depends on.

Chapter 15

The 30-Day Digital Estate Plan

By this point, you know enough to make the plan. That does not mean you need to finish everything tonight.

A digital estate plan becomes much easier when you stop treating it like one giant project and start treating it like a series of small decisions. The problem is not that any single task is impossible. The problem is that the whole subject arrives at once. Accounts, passwords, email, phones, photos, subscriptions, legal documents, crypto, business systems, privacy, family, advisers. It is too much when viewed as one block.

So do not view it as one block.

Give yourself thirty days.

Not thirty full days of work. Thirty ordinary days with a small task attached to each one. Some tasks will take ten minutes. Some may take half an hour. A few may take longer if your life is complex. The point is not to move fast. The point is to build momentum without turning the plan into another thing you resent.

You are not trying to complete a perfect estate system in a month. You are trying to create a useful first version. A first version that tells someone where to begin, what matters, what not to cancel, who to call and where sensitive instructions are stored. That alone would put you ahead of most people.

A finished first version is better than an imagined perfect one.

The 30-day plan below is designed to be practical. It starts with visibility, then moves into access, preservation, money, public identity, high-risk assets, people, storage and review. If a day does not apply to you, skip it. If a task needs more time, mark it and keep going. Do not let one hard item stop the whole plan.

The rule is simple: do the next useful thing.

Week One: Make the Invisible Visible

The first week is about finding the shape of your digital life. Do not try to solve everything yet. Just make the major parts visible.

Day 1: Create the master document

Create a document, spreadsheet or worksheet called "Digital Estate Inventory." Add the date at the top. Add the core headings: category, item, why it matters, where to find it, access method, first action and notes.

Do not worry about formatting. Do not search for the perfect template. The first version can be plain. It only needs to exist.

At the top, write: "This inventory is a map, not a password list. Sensitive access material is stored separately."

That sentence sets the tone for the entire plan.

Day 2: List your primary accounts

Write down your main email account, phone number, password manager, Apple ID, Google account, Microsoft account, main bank, main cloud storage account and main computer or phone. These are the accounts and devices most likely to control everything else.

For each one, add a short note explaining why it matters. For example: "Primary Gmail account used for banking, subscriptions and password resets." Or: "iPhone receives two-factor authentication codes."

This is not detailed planning yet. It is orientation.

Day 3: List your devices

List phones, laptops, tablets, desktops, external drives, old phones, USB drives, hardware security keys and any smart home systems that matter. Mark the devices that receive authentication codes, hold photos, store files or control other accounts.

Add one warning if relevant: "Do not wipe or reset this device until photos, files and authentication apps have been reviewed."

That one warning may prevent a permanent mistake.

Day 4: List your email accounts

Write down every email address you currently use or know is still attached to something important. Include old email accounts, business email, work email, Apple ID, Google accounts and email addresses used for banking, investments or social media.

Mark the gateway email accounts. These are the accounts that can reset passwords, reveal statements or control other services.

If an old email account still matters, do not ignore it just because you rarely use it.

Day 5: List your cloud storage and photo locations

Write down where your photos, videos and important files live. Include iCloud Photos, Google Photos, OneDrive, Dropbox, external drives, old laptops, WhatsApp, shared albums and social media accounts.

Choose the closest thing to your main photo archive and write it clearly: "My main photo archive is stored in [location]. Preserve this before closing accounts or wiping devices."

Your family may care about this more than almost anything else.

Day 6: List financial providers

Write down banks, credit cards, loans, mortgages, investment platforms, retirement or superannuation accounts, insurance providers, tax accounts, payment apps and business financial systems.

Do not record balances unless you have a specific reason. Balances change. Providers matter more.

The goal is to make sure accounts can be found.

Day 7: Stop and review the first week

Read what you have created. Do not fix everything. Just ask whether someone could already understand the broad shape of your digital life better than they could a week ago.

They probably could.

That matters.

Week Two: Protect Access Without Exposing Everything

The second week is about access paths. This is where people often either do nothing or create a dangerous password list. You are aiming for the middle: enough direction to recover important accounts, without turning the inventory into a master key.

Day 8: Review your password system

Write down which password manager or password method you use. If you use a password manager, record the name and where emergency access instructions are stored. If you do not use one, write that honestly and mark it as an improvement area.

Do not write your master password in the inventory.

If your passwords are scattered across browsers, notes, memory and paper, do not panic. The goal is to make the current system visible before improving it.

Day 9: Identify gateway accounts

From your inventory, mark accounts that unlock other accounts. These will likely include primary email, password manager, phone, Apple ID, Google account, authenticator app, banking email, cloud storage and business email.

Gateway accounts deserve stronger protection and clearer recovery instructions.

If one account controls everything, make that obvious.

Day 10: Review two-factor authentication

For each gateway account, record the type of two-factor authentication used. SMS, authenticator app, push notification, password manager code, hardware key, backup codes or email code.

Do not write the codes in the inventory. Write where the secure recovery instructions are stored.

If your phone is the only second factor for multiple accounts, mark your phone plan as critical.

Day 11: Create a "do not cancel yet" list

List services that should not be cancelled immediately after death or incapacity. Common examples include phone plan, cloud storage, password manager, domain names, email hosting, website hosting, accounting software, business tools and backup services.

This list is one of the most useful parts of the entire plan. It tells people what to preserve before they start tidying up.

Day 12: Create a secure access note

Create a separate secure note, sealed envelope or password manager entry that explains where sensitive access instructions live. This might include password manager emergency access, device recovery, backup codes, safe location or lawyer-held instructions.

Keep this separate from the general inventory.

The inventory should point to it. It should not expose it.

Day 13: Label physical items

Label important physical items in a way that helps without revealing secrets. External drives, old phones, hardware wallets, backup envelopes, legal folders, USB drives and recovery documents should not look like random clutter.

A label such as "Family photo archive" or "Do not reset. Read digital estate instructions first" can prevent accidental loss.

Day 14: Review access boundaries

Write down any privacy boundaries. Private messages, journals, personal folders, business client files, medical documents, old devices or sensitive folders may need specific instructions.

Do not leave people guessing what they are allowed to open.

A sentence is often enough.

Week Three: Reduce Financial and Practical Confusion

The third week is about money, subscriptions, business systems and public accounts. This is where you prevent waste, missed assets, broken services and awkward public loose ends.

Day 15: Review recurring payments

Check bank statements, credit card statements, PayPal, Apple subscriptions, Google subscriptions and business card statements. List recurring payments and annual renewals.

Mark each one as cancel, preserve temporarily, transfer, review or critical.

Pay special attention to services that store data or support access.

Day 16: Review insurance and tax records

List insurance providers, policy types, where documents are stored and who should be contacted. Then list tax records, accountant details, accounting software and investment tax document locations.

This is not glamorous work. It is the kind of information families are grateful to find.

Day 17: List income sources

Write down any places where money may continue to arrive. Salary, business revenue, rental income, dividends, royalties, Amazon KDP, Stripe, PayPal, Shopify, affiliate programs, advertising revenue, paid communities or marketplace accounts.

A bank account shows where money lands. It does not always show where money comes from.

Day 18: List debts and obligations

Record loans, credit cards, tax debts, buy-now-pay-later accounts, business debts, leases, contracts, memberships, annual renewals and service agreements.

Do not hide obligations because they are uncomfortable. The estate will have to deal with them eventually. Better to leave a clear path.

Day 19: Review social media and public profiles

List accounts where people can find you, follow you, contact you or see your work. Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, X, YouTube, TikTok, Medium, Substack, personal websites, business websites, forums, communities and professional directories.

For each account, choose a likely action: preserve, memorialise, delete, download archive, transfer, update profile or review before deciding.

Day 20: Review websites, domains and public identity

List domain names, hosting providers, website platforms, email providers and renewal methods. Mark any domain that should not be allowed to expire.

If a domain controls email, business identity, published work or customer access, treat it as important.

Small annual renewals can protect large digital systems.

Day 21: Review business and creator systems

If you run a business, publish work or operate online platforms, list the systems that must not fail in the first week. Email, website, bookings, payroll, accounting, payment processors, customer records, supplier records, social accounts and critical subscriptions.

If you do not run a business, use this day to catch up.

Week Four: People, Storage and Final Review

The fourth week turns the inventory into a usable plan. Accounts matter, but people use the plan. Storage matters, because a plan that cannot be found is almost the same as no plan.

Day 22: Identify legal decision-makers

Write down the person or people named in your estate documents, or the people who should speak with a lawyer about those roles. Executor, attorney, trustee, business director, guardian, adviser.

If you have not created formal estate documents, write that down as a gap to address with a professional.

The digital plan does not replace legal authority.

Day 23: Identify trusted helpers

List people who can help with specific areas: devices, photos, business systems, cloud storage, password manager, social media, crypto, domains, family communication, tax or legal processes.

Match people to tasks. Do not assume one person should carry everything.

Day 24: Set default rules

Write a short list of default rules for unclear situations. Preserve first, decide later. Use official provider processes for financial accounts. Do not cancel critical subscriptions until reviewed. Do not reset devices until files and authentication have been checked. Do not review private material unless necessary.

Defaults help people act calmly when they are unsure.

Day 25: Choose where the plan lives

Decide the starting point. Estate folder, home safe, lawyer file, secure cloud folder, password manager note, printed "Read This First" document or hybrid system.

Write: "If something happens to me, start with [location]."

Make the beginning easy.

Day 26: Separate sensitive material

Confirm that passwords, seed phrases, private keys, recovery codes and sensitive access instructions are not sitting casually inside the general inventory.

If sensitive material exists, store it securely and make sure the inventory points to the secure process.

The map and the keys should not be the same document.

Day 27: Tell the right person

Have one conversation. Tell a trusted person that the plan exists and where to begin. They do not need every detail. They need to know that there is a plan and what their role might be.

This is the part many people avoid.

It is also one of the most important parts.

Day 28: Review the first page

Create or improve the first page of the plan. It should tell someone:

  • where to begin
  • who to contact
  • what not to cancel
  • which devices to preserve
  • where the inventory is
  • where secure instructions are stored
  • which items are high-risk
  • what the first three actions are

If the first page works, the rest of the plan becomes easier.

Day 29: Remove clutter

Delete unnecessary entries. Remove old notes. Mark uncertain items clearly. Make the plan easier to scan. Highlight critical items. Check that the language is plain.

A shorter plan people can use is better than a longer plan they avoid.

Day 30: Date it and set the review

Add the created date, last reviewed date and next review date. Put the next review in your calendar. Add a small change log. Save the master version. Remove outdated copies or mark them clearly as superseded.

Then stop.

The first version is done.

Not perfect. Useful.

That is the goal.

What You Should Have After 30 Days

At the end of this process, you should have a digital estate inventory that lists the main accounts, devices, files, services, people and instructions your family would need. You should know where your photos are. You should know which email accounts matter. You should know which phone plan should not be cancelled too quickly. You should know where passwords are managed. You should know which subscriptions support important systems. You should know who can help.

You should also have something more valuable than a document: a calmer view of your own digital life.

You will probably notice old accounts you can close, subscriptions you can cancel, files you can organise, passwords you can improve and people you need to speak with. That is part of the benefit. Digital estate planning is not only about death. It is about reducing the hidden mess while you are alive.

If you finish the thirty days and still have gaps, that is normal. Some gaps require legal advice. Some require conversations. Some require technical help. Some require decisions you are not ready to make yet.

Write them down.

A known gap is better than an invisible one.

The plan does not have to be complete to be useful. If your family can find the main accounts, preserve the right devices, avoid cancelling critical services, recover photos, contact advisers and understand who should help, you have already reduced the burden significantly.

That is what this book has been building toward.

Not a perfect archive of your digital life.

A practical path through it.

Schedule one session at the end of each week:

  • Week one review
  • Week two review
  • Week three review
  • Week four review

These are short checkpoints, not long planning retreats.

4. Choose your trusted person

Write the name of the person who should know the plan exists.

If you are not ready to tell them everything, tell them only where the plan begins.

5. Identify your likely hard task

Choose the task you are most likely to avoid:

  • passwords
  • legal documents
  • crypto
  • family conversation
  • old email accounts
  • business systems
  • privacy instructions
  • subscriptions
  • photo archive
  • storage location

Mark it now. Avoidance is easier to manage when you can see it.

6. Define done

Write what "done for now" means.

Example:

"My first version is done when it lists my primary email, phone, password manager, financial providers, photo archive, critical subscriptions, trusted helpers, storage location and first-page instructions."

Done for now is enough.

7. Set the review date

Write:

"My next review date will be [date]."

A plan without a review date is already beginning to age.

8. Write the one-sentence commitment

Finish with one clear sentence:

"I am not trying to document everything. I am creating a clear first version that helps the right people find what matters, protect what matters and avoid the mistakes that are hardest to reverse."

That is the whole project. Everything else is detail.

Conclusion

Leave Less Confusion Behind

Most people will not create a digital estate plan because they are thinking about death. They will create one because, at some point, they realise how much of their life has become invisible to everyone else.

That is the real shift.

Your bank may not send paper statements. Your photos may not sit in albums. Your most important documents may not live in a filing cabinet. Your memories may be spread across cloud folders, phones, old drives and messaging apps. Your business may depend on subscriptions no one else understands. Your public identity may live on platforms your family does not use. Your accounts may be protected by passwords, devices, recovery codes and two-factor authentication systems designed, quite reasonably, to keep other people out.

While you are alive, this can feel normal. You know enough to get by. You know which phone matters, which email account to search, which folder holds the document, which subscription supports the website, which cloud account stores the photos, which person to call when something breaks. Even if the system is messy, it is your mess, and you carry much of it in your head.

The difficulty is that your head is not an estate plan.

If something happens to you, the people left behind do not inherit your memory of how everything fits together. They inherit the accounts, devices, subscriptions, files, payment trails, public profiles and locked doors. They inherit the practical work of finding what matters while dealing with grief, paperwork, family responsibilities and decisions they may not feel ready to make.

This book has not argued for a perfect system. Perfect systems are fragile because they depend on perfect maintenance, perfect behaviour and perfect future conditions. Real life is not like that. People change phones. Passwords change. Families change. Businesses change. Platforms change. What matters is not perfection. What matters is leaving enough structure that the right people can begin.

A useful digital estate plan helps people find what matters, access only what should be accessed and know what action to take next. Find. Access. Action. Those three words are deliberately plain because the work itself should be plain.

Your family does not need a beautifully designed archive of your entire digital life. They need to know where the main email account is, which phone should not be wiped, where the photos are stored, which subscriptions should not be cancelled too quickly, which providers should be contacted formally, who can help with technical access, where sensitive instructions live and what material should remain private. They need fewer locked doors, fewer guesses and fewer irreversible mistakes.

There is kindness in that. Not dramatic kindness. Not the kind people talk about in grand language. A quieter kind. The kind that says: I thought about this before you had to. I did not leave everything in my head. I did not expect you to become a detective while grieving. I left a path.

That path does not need to reveal everything. In fact, it should not. Privacy still matters. Security still matters. Legal authority still matters. A good plan does not hand every secret to everyone. It separates the map from the keys. It tells people what exists without exposing every password. It points to secure instructions without turning the inventory into a risk. It gives helpers roles without giving them unlimited authority.

The aim is not to make your digital life public. The aim is to make it manageable.

If you have read this far, you may already see your own digital life differently. You may be thinking about the old email account that still controls something important, the phone plan that receives banking codes, the cloud account holding family photos, the domain that renews once a year, the subscription nobody should cancel immediately, the password manager no one else knows exists, the files that would be painful to lose, the private material that deserves clearer boundaries.

That awareness is not a burden. It is the beginning of control.

You do not need to fix everything at once. You only need to make the next sensible improvement. Create the inventory. List the gateway accounts. Mark the critical subscriptions. Identify the photo archive. Tell one trusted person where the plan begins. Move the sensitive material out of casual storage. Write down what should not be deleted. Add a review date.

Small actions compound here, just as they do everywhere else.

A one-page plan is better than silence. A rough inventory is better than a perfect idea you never start. A labelled hard drive is better than an unexplained one. A note saying "do not cancel this phone plan yet" may prevent hours of account recovery problems. A sentence about private folders may protect something you care about. A named technical helper may spare your executor from feeling lost. A simple "Read This First" page may be the difference between calm and chaos.

Digital estate planning can sound cold because it deals with accounts, passwords, files and systems. But underneath all of that, it is deeply human. It is about photos people want back. It is about money that should not disappear. It is about businesses that should not fail because one person held all the access. It is about memories that should not be trapped on a locked phone. It is about privacy, dignity and judgement. It is about helping the people who will one day have to act when you cannot.

You are not trying to control everything from beyond the grave. That would be impossible, and probably unfair. The future will still require judgement. Your family will still have to make decisions. Advisers may still need to be contacted. Providers will still have processes. Some things may still be hard.

The plan simply makes the hard things less blind. It gives people a starting point, permission to pause before cancelling or deleting, names, locations, boundaries and enough confidence to take the first step.

One day, every digital life becomes someone else's practical problem. That sounds blunt, but it is true. The question is whether we leave that problem as a locked room full of unlabelled boxes, or as a set of instructions written with care.

Choose the second.

Leave the map.

Protect the keys.

Tell the right person where to begin.

And leave less confusion behind.
